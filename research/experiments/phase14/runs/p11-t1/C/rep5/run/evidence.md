## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the credit-card minimum-payment autopay option cut exact-minimum payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce debt, because nudged users chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum and cash-strapped users disengaged from autopay.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). placeholder

**Why it matters here.** Directly undermines the 'defaults always help' premise in a consumer financial product: a nominally successful nudge changed enrolment behaviour without changing the underlying outcome, exactly the 'vanity enrolment' risk support raised.

**Method.** Randomised field experiment on live credit-card accounts, six-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- credit-card debt paydown, not savings accumulation
- effect measured at 6 months only, not 12

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Defaults in wealth-management advice significantly shift risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes, but implementing a default (or overriding one) lowers the client's rating of the advice received, an effect mediated by perceived advisor honesty; framing of the default question (how much to invest vs. how much to leave safe) also shifts allocations.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). EU consumer financial-advice experiment on defaults and framing that directly informs the manipulation/dark-pattern and anchoring questions the brief raises.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests, in an EU consumer-financial-advice setting, exactly the manipulation/reputational-risk trade-off the brief flags: a default that shifts behavior can also erode trust, and how the default and preset level are framed changes both compliance and perceived honesty -- this should shape how the round-up multiplier default is presented, not just which value is chosen.

**Method.** Three experiments including a large representative sample across five European countries (n=4207) on default and framing manipulations in investment advice.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management investment advice rather than a round-up savings feature
- no persistence data beyond the experimental session
- risk-taking outcome, not enrollment/contribution behavior

## 3. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults raised donations by over $43 million but also increased requested refunds by almost $3 million, with larger effects on smaller and less-experienced donors who did not compensate by changing donation amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest empirical demonstration that a recurring-financial-commitment default can produce unintended, regretted enrolment, direct evidence for the manipulation/dark-pattern risk the brief flags.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a default can move nominal financial commitment (recurring donations) without users intending it, evidenced by elevated refund requests - precisely the 'passively enrolled, didn't understand what they signed up for' failure mode the support team worries about for round-up enrolment.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across political campaign websites around the 2020 US election.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a savings product
- US election context, not the EU consumer-protection regulatory setting

## 4. The Effect of Choice Screens on Mobile Browser Usage: Evidence from the EU Digital Markets Act [UNVERIFIED — check manually]

Jesper Akesson, Kush Amlani, Raul Cepeda Suarez, Emily Chissell et al. · 2026 · Social Science Research Network · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** After the EU Digital Markets Act mandated browser choice screens, Firefox usage rose 113% on iOS and 12% on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual, with effects observed from 15 months after rollout onward.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). The closest available natural experiment on active choice versus default in an EU-regulated mobile-app setting, directly bearing on the active-choice-vs-default decision.

**Limitations.**

- outcome is browser usage, not financial enrolment or savings amount
- platform-level natural experiment, not a within-app active-choice test

## 5. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Steady-state saving rates increase by only 0.6% of income due to automatic enrollment and 0.3% due to default auto-escalation once job turnover, withdrawal on separation, and opt-outs are accounted for; only 40% of auto-escalation defaults escalate on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that default enrollment reliably raises saving once medium/long-run dynamics are modeled, with numeric effect sizes.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of our support team's objection: the enrolment lift from opt-out defaults shrinks dramatically once medium-run attrition and disengagement are modeled, directly bearing on whether an opt-out round-up default would be vanity enrollment.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating employee turnover, balance withdrawal, and auto-escalation opt-out dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated context absent from our product
- auto-escalation dynamic differs from a fixed round-up multiplier

## 7. Defaults and effortful tasks

Lars Behlen, Oliver Himmler, Robert Jäckle · 2023 · Experimental Economics · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1007/s10683-023-09808-8>

**Key finding.** Opt-out defaults increased sign-up in both single-exam and many-exam settings, but only translated into greater effortful task completion (participation and passing) for the single, strongly-endorsed default and only among previously responsive individuals; for many exams and non-responsive individuals there was no effect on actual outcomes despite higher sign-up.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Directly tests whether an opt-out default that raises sign-up also raises real engagement, the core mechanism behind the brief's disengagement worry, making it the closest available evidence on that specific question despite the non-financial setting.

**Why it matters here.** The most direct evidence available that a default can raise nominal enrolment without increasing the effortful behaviour it's meant to produce unless targeted at people already inclined to engage, exactly the 'passively enrolled users don't engage' concern the support team raises about round-up savings.

**Method.** Two field experiments changing university exam sign-up to opt-out, tracking downstream effortful outcomes (participation, passing).

**Limitations.**

- university exam sign-up, not a financial savings product
- effort/engagement measured through exam behaviour, not money saved or withdrawn

## 8. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale automatic enrolment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts at UK banks cut unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, worth an estimated £170-240 million market-wide.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Directly analogous consumer-banking automatic-enrolment field experiment, the nearest real-world precedent to the round-up feature itself.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world precedent for auto-enrolling consumers into a banking-app feature by default rather than by choice, in the same consumer-financial, self-directed setting as the round-up feature - evidence that opt-out defaults inside a bank app can produce real financial benefit, not just nominal sign-up.

**Method.** Large-scale field experiments run directly inside UK bank consumer accounts.

**Limitations.**

- measures downstream financial outcomes (overdraft charges) rather than enrolment or contribution-level persistence directly
- no comparison against opt-in or active-choice enrolment

## 9. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrolment raised participation above 85% at three firms, but about 80% of participants initially accepted the low default contribution rate (2-3%) and default conservative fund, with half still anchored on the default after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest prior work quantifying both the default-enrolment effect and, specifically, anchoring at a preset contribution level over multiple years — directly informing the multiplier pre-set decision.

**Why it matters here.** Closest prior work directly quantifying anchoring on a preset default contribution level and its persistence over years, the exact evidence needed for the C3 decision on whether to pre-set a round-up multiplier.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative data analysis from three large US firms comparing automatic versus voluntary 401(k) enrolment.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored 401(k) plan with employer as trusted intermediary, the mechanism the brief says does not carry over
- illiquid long-horizon retirement savings versus liquid round-up sweeps
- 2001 data predates app-based fintech and current EU regulatory norms
