## 1. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a representative five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges shift risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes, but implementing a default (or having it refused) lowers ratings of the advisor's honesty and advice quality, and how the default is framed and presented also shifts the chosen allocation.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU-based experimental evidence on default framing effects on chosen level and on perceived manipulation, directly speaking to the brief's dark-pattern concern.

**Why it matters here.** Gives EU consumer-context evidence that defaults can anchor the chosen level (relevant to the preset-multiplier decision) while also measurably damaging perceived trust when a default is imposed or refused \u2014 the exact 'dark pattern' reputational risk the brief flags.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, one large representative multi-country European sample, on default framing in investment/wealth-management decisions.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management advice context, not a round-up savings app
- risk-taking allocation rather than a savings-amount outcome
- no medium/long-term persistence data

## 2. The Effect of Choice Screens on Mobile Browser Usage: Evidence from the EU Digital Markets Act [UNVERIFIED — check manually]

Jesper Akesson, Kush Amlani, Raul Cepeda Suarez, Emily Chissell et al. · 2026 · Social Science Research Network · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** After the EU Digital Markets Act mandated active-choice browser screens replacing preset defaults, Firefox usage rose 113% on iOS and 12% on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual, with effects persisting 15+ months after rollout.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). The strongest available evidence on active choice versus default persistence at scale in an EU-regulated consumer digital product, directly bearing on the entry-design decision.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest large-scale, long-horizon evidence that a regulator-mandated active-choice screen can substantially and durably shift outcomes away from a preset default in a digital consumer product, directly informing whether active choice would meaningfully move enrolment relative to opt-in/opt-out and whether the effect fades.

**Method.** Difference-in-differences design exploiting the staggered rollout of EU DMA-mandated choice screens on iOS and Android.

**Limitations.**

- Browser choice, not a financial savings product
- Measures usage share, not amount saved or engagement depth

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Across nine 401(k) plans, steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from automatic enrollment and 0.3% from default auto-escalation once job turnover, pre-vesting withdrawals, and opt-outs from escalation are accounted for; only 40% of those defaulted into auto-escalation actually escalate on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that default enrollment reliably translates into materially more saving, with quantified effect sizes.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of the support team's objection: naive enrollment-lift numbers substantially overstate the real, medium-run increase in money saved once attrition and opt-out are accounted for.

**Method.** Longitudinal administrative analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated context the brief says doesn't transfer
- no direct measure of engagement or complaints

## 4. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment that made an autopay-minimum default less convenient reduced minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because nudged cardholders mostly chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum and some stopped using autopay at all, increasing missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Directly tests whether a choice-architecture nudge that changes headline behavior also changes the substantive financial outcome, in a genuine consumer financial product.

**Why it matters here.** This is the closest real-world proof that a choice-architecture change can move a nominal enrollment/behavior metric while leaving the underlying outcome (debt, and by analogy, saved amount) unchanged or worse \u2014 exactly the 'vanity enrollment' risk our support team is warning about, in an actual consumer banking product rather than a pension.

**Method.** Field experiment with card issuer, six-month follow-up on payment behavior and debt outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- studies debt repayment nudges, not savings sign-up
- US credit card population, not EU app users
- the specific nudge (shrouding) differs from opt-in/opt-out/active choice framing

## 6. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults on political campaign websites increased donations by over $43 million while also increasing refund requests by almost $3 million, with the largest impact on smaller, less-experienced donors who did not compensate by changing donation amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best-quantified real-world evidence of a recurring-commitment default producing both enrolment gains and manipulation-like backlash, closely analogous to the round-up opt-out risk.

**Why it matters here.** Directly demonstrates the mechanism the brief's support team worries about: a default creating an unintended recurring commitment lifts nominal sign-up but generates disproportionate refunds/complaints among smaller, less experienced users, the clearest quantified analogue to round-up opt-out risk.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design exploiting different rollout timing of pre-checked recurring-donation defaults across 2020 U.S. campaign websites.

**Limitations.**

- Political donations, not a savings product
- No 6-12 month engagement or withdrawal outcomes reported

## 7. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · computational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model of 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions finds default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out cost (84% of adherence needs both), and a counterfactual adding a 15% tip option below the existing 20/25/30% menu would capture 69% of the achievable consumer welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Directly analogous structural evidence on how preset menu options anchor consumer choice, in a self-directed digital consumer payment setting.

**Why it matters here.** The closest available quantitative precedent for the preset-multiplier decision: it shows how a menu of preset options (analogous to 1x/2x/5x) anchors choice through friction rather than reshaping preference, and quantifies the welfare cost/benefit of adding or changing menu options \u2014 directly informing what levels to preset and whether an empty field would perform differently.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on large-scale transaction data plus an independent stated-preference survey to identify true preferences.

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions, not savings contributions
- consumer payment app setting, not a savings product specifically
- no persistence or long-run engagement outcomes

## 8. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In a 220-participant RCT on voluntary pension saving decisions in China, behavioural nudges including default options significantly reduced willingness to contribute and the share of income allocated to saving, contrary to prevailing international evidence, with the largest backfire effects for positive-narrative and default-option nudges.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest contradicting evidence in the set, directly answering the brief's request for a paper contesting the 'defaults always help' premise.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the brief's working assumption that defaults reliably lift enrolment, the most explicit counter-evidence in the shortlist that a default can backfire and reduce contribution, with heterogeneity by income, education, and gender.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey testing social-norm, framing, default-option, and narrative nudges on voluntary pension contribution decisions (n=220).

**Limitations.**

- Hypothetical online-survey decisions rather than a live product
- Small sample (n=220) and a non-EU, non-fintech cultural context (China)

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A field experiment with payment reminders reduced credit card late fees by 14% but increased overdraft fees by 9%, with users who had a history of overdraft use experiencing a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Best available quantified evidence in this batch for the brief's explicit concern about who is helped versus hurt by a nudge in a consumer financial product.

**Why it matters here.** Gives quantified evidence that a well-intentioned nudge can help most users while making a specific, identifiable subgroup worse off — directly bears on the brief's requirement not to ship a default that looks good on average while harming vulnerable users, and argues for monitoring heterogeneous effects (e.g. by liquidity/overdraft history) rather than a single enrolment metric.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment with a financial management platform in Brazil; fee outcomes tracked across credit card and checking accounts.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrolment defaults or preset contribution levels
- credit card/overdraft fee context rather than round-up savings
- no data on persistence beyond the fee-measurement period
