## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the credit-card minimum-payment autopay option cut minimum-only payers by 23% at six months but did not reduce debt, and nudged autopay enrollees chose amounts only slightly above the minimum.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Closest empirical evidence that a default/nudge can lift a headline metric while leaving real behavior (and disengagement/complaint risk) largely unchanged, with anchoring on the default level.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a nudge/default can move nominal enrollment behavior without changing the substantive outcome and that people anchor near the preset default level — the clearest empirical case for our support team's 'vanity enrollment' worry and for anchoring risk on a preset multiplier.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment on credit card autopay enrollment with 6-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- US credit-card debt context, not a savings product
- effect measured on minimum-payment status, not enrollment into a savings feature

## 2. The Effect of Choice Screens on Mobile Browser Usage: Evidence from the EU Digital Markets Act [UNVERIFIED — check manually]

Jesper Akesson, Kush Amlani, Raul Cepeda Suarez, Emily Chissell et al. · 2026 · Social Science Research Network · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** Mandated active-choice browser screens under the EU Digital Markets Act produced large, durable shifts in usage — Firefox usage 113% higher on iOS and 12% higher on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual, persisting 15+ months after rollout.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest large-scale, persistent, real-world test of active choice against defaults under EU regulation, directly informing the entry-mechanism decision.

**Why it matters here.** Directly answers whether forcing an active choice can produce a large, persistent shift in behavior comparable to a default — the central evidence needed to decide whether active choice captures most of the enrollment benefit without a default's disengagement risk, in an EU regulatory context.

**Method.** Difference-in-differences design exploiting the staggered EU DMA-mandated rollout of browser choice screens on iOS and Android.

**Limitations.**

- studies default browser selection, not financial enrollment or contribution amounts
- no data on whether the chosen option is actually used/engaged with beyond usage share

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrollment and default auto-escalation: steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of auto-escalated employees escalate on the first date, with more opting out later.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably deliver large, durable saving increases, with concrete effect sizes the brief explicitly asked for.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of our support team's objection: default-driven enrollment lift shrinks dramatically once realistic attrition and opt-out dynamics are included, directly bearing on whether opt-out enrollment numbers would be vanity.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans accounting for job turnover, balance withdrawal on separation, and auto-escalation opt-out.

**Limitations.**

- retirement (401k) plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated enrollment channel differs from a self-directed app

## 4. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments, including a representative sample from five European countries (n=4207), default nudges shifted risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes but lowered ratings of the wealth manager's advice compared to no default, especially among those who refused the default; framing of the default presentation had smaller but significant effects on allocation.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Directly addresses the EU dark-pattern/manipulation trade-off in a consumer financial-advice setting closely analogous to ours.

**Why it matters here.** EU-based evidence that a default can carry a real reputational and perceived-manipulation cost, and that how the default is framed and presented changes its effect — directly informs whether our round-up multiplier default reads as helpful or as a dark pattern.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments on investment-allocation defaults, including a large representative multi-country European experiment.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management/investment-allocation context rather than a savings round-up feature
- advice-rating outcome, not enrollment or amount saved

## 5. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation boxes raised campaign donations by over $43 million but also raised refund requests by almost $3 million, with the largest impact on smaller, less-experienced donors who apparently did not intend the recurring commitment.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly quantifies the backfire/regret cost of a pre-checked recurring commitment default, the same mechanism as auto-enrolling round-ups, with a distributional skew toward vulnerable users.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world precedent for a pre-checked recurring-contribution default: it moves nominal sign-ups a lot, but a measurable share of that is unintended and gets reversed, and the effect concentrates on inexperienced/small users — precisely the disengagement and dark-pattern risk our support team is worried about.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design exploiting the timing of pre-checked recurring-donation defaults across 2020 US campaign websites.

**Limitations.**

- political donations rather than a bank savings feature
- refund requests are a proxy for regret, not a direct withdrawal/abandonment measure for a savings pot

## 7. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In a 220-participant RCT on voluntary pension saving in China, default-option nudges (along with social-norm and framing nudges) significantly reduced willingness to contribute relative to control, with default and positive-narrative nudges producing the strongest backfire, and effects varying by income, education, and gender.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest contradicting evidence in the batch that a default can reduce rather than increase saving intentions, with explicit distributional heterogeneity.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the internal premise that 'defaults will lift enrolment' — here a default nudge backfires and reduces willingness to contribute, with heterogeneous effects across demographic groups, exactly the counter-evidence the brief asked us to surface before committing to opt-out.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey testing behavioral nudges including default options on voluntary pension contribution decisions.

**Limitations.**

- hypothetical willingness-to-contribute survey rather than real transactions
- voluntary pension saving in China, a different cultural/regulatory context than an EU consumer banking app
- small sample (220 participants)

## 8. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale field experiments at major UK banks show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts cut unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, worth an estimated £170-240 million market-wide, though consumers still captured less than half of available savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Provides a rare consumer-banking-app, at-scale field experiment on automatic enrollment vs. opt-in with quantified financial outcomes, exactly the setting the brief is missing evidence for.

**Why it matters here.** This is the closest real analogue to our decision: automatic enrollment vs. opt-in for a feature inside a consumer banking app, with real financial outcomes rather than survey intentions, and it shows defaults deliver only partial realized benefit even when they clearly move enrollment.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments run with UK retail banks on live customer accounts.

**Limitations.**

- outcome is alert receipt/overdraft avoidance, not savings enrollment or amount saved
- no explicit 6-12 month persistence breakdown given in the abstract

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A payment-reminder nudge in a consumer credit-card/checking platform cut late-payment fees by 14% overall but raised overdraft fees by 9%, with users who had a history of overdraft use experiencing a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Strong consumer-fintech RCT evidence of heterogeneous nudge effects, directly informing the distributional/manipulation-risk question the brief flags as essential before building.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the brief's 'who is helped and who is hurt' concern: a well-intentioned nudge in a consumer fintech setting produced a subgroup that was made worse off, which is exactly the risk the support team raises about defaulting everyone into round-up savings — some users may be net harmed even as average enrolment or engagement rises.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment with a financial management platform in Brazil, comparing reminder vs. no-reminder groups on credit card late fees and checking-account overdraft fees.

**Limitations.**

- studies reminder nudges, not enrolment defaults or preset contribution levels
- credit card/overdraft fees rather than savings enrolment or amounts
- Brazilian market, not EU consumer-protection context
- abstract gives no 6-12 month persistence data
