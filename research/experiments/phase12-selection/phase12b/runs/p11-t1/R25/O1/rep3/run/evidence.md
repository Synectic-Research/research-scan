## 1. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a 4,207-person sample from five European countries, default nudges shift investment risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes but also lower clients' rating of the advisor's advice, especially when the default is one the client ends up refusing, with perceived honesty mediating the effect.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). European, consumer-facing experimental evidence on when a default is perceived as helpful versus manipulative -- precisely the reputational-risk question the brief raises, and it touches the preset-level anchoring question too.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the EU manipulation-risk trade-off named in the brief: even an effective default can damage perceived trustworthiness and be read as manipulative, which argues for active choice or individualized defaults over a one-size-fits-all opt-out multiplier, and cautions on how the pre-set level is framed.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, including a large representative multi-country European experiment, on default framing in investment/risk-taking decisions.

**Limitations.**

- Wealth-management/investment advice setting rather than a round-up savings toggle
- advice-rating outcome, not actual retention or complaint data

## 2. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the autopay-minimum option cut minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit-card debt, because nudged users chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Consumer financial nudge field experiment showing enrollment change without amount change and heterogeneous harm to liquidity-constrained users -- near-identical mechanism to the round-up decision.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests the brief's core worry: a nudge changed nominal enrollment behavior (23% fewer minimum-only payers) without moving the underlying financial outcome, and reduced autopay enrollment increased missed payments among liquidity-constrained users -- exactly the enrolment-without-engagement pattern support flagged.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit-card accounts tracking payment behavior over six months (Guttman-Kenney et al., NBER 2023).

**Limitations.**

- Credit-card debt paydown, not savings enrollment
- shrouding is a different manipulation than opt-in/opt-out/active-choice framing

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Across nine 401(k) plans, steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from automatic enrollment and 0.3% from default auto-escalation once turnover, pre-vesting withdrawals, and opt-outs are accounted for; only 40% of auto-escalation defaults escalate on the first scheduled date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The tracking-attrition methodology (measuring nominal enrollment/escalation against actual retained, active saving over time) is the direct method transfer for auditing whether round-up 'enrollment' numbers reflect real engagement.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantitative rebuttal to 'defaults always help': the same erosion mechanisms -- disengagement, opt-out over time, and withdrawal of nominal balances -- apply directly to a liquid round-up pot, where switching/withdrawal costs are even lower than in a 401(k), so the true long-run lift from opt-out enrollment is likely smaller than the headline enrollment number suggests.

**Method.** Longitudinal administrative-data analysis of nine 401(k) plans tracking enrollment, escalation, and withdrawal dynamics over years.

**Limitations.**

- Employer 401(k) plans with vesting and job-turnover dynamics not present in a banking app
- no consumer product engagement or complaint data

## 4. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · computational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model of 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions finds default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out cost (84% of adherence needs both frictions together), and adding a lower preset option (15%) to an existing 20/25/30% menu captures 69% of the achievable consumer-welfare gain from full customization at only 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). A consumer, self-directed, app-like default-menu setting with a rigorous mechanism and welfare analysis of preset-level design -- closest available evidence for the multiplier question.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a transferable mechanism and welfare-tradeoff framework for exactly the multiplier-menu decision: it shows how adding one more preset level near what people actually want can recover most of the welfare gain of a blank field, without abandoning defaults -- directly informs whether to ship 1x/2x/5x or something denser, and shows opt-out friction (not just anchoring) drives adherence.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on 8.6M transaction records plus an independent stated-preference survey of true preferences, with counterfactual simulation.

**Limitations.**

- Tipping decisions on a single transaction, not a repeated savings enrollment choice
- no engagement/withdrawal outcomes over time

## 5. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework showing that downstream effects of defaults depend on the time course of choice/consumption, prior preferences, and salience of trade-offs, and that findings on persistence are currently inconsistent across studies.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Most direct general-consumer treatment of exactly the persistence/downstream-effects question the brief raises, independent of the employer-pension literature.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the moderator variables (time course, prior preference strength, trade-off salience) that should shape what we measure at 6-12 months to know whether round-up enrollment is a durable behavior change or just inertia.

**Method.** Conceptual/theoretical review synthesizing consumer-default literature into a taxonomy and research agenda. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- Theoretical/synthesis paper, not new empirical estimates
- not specific to financial products

## 6. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts reduces unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, but consumers still capture less than half of the potential savings available from cheaper liquidity sources.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Closest available analog: automatic enrollment in a UK consumer banking app with quantified financial outcomes and heterogeneity in who benefits.

**Why it matters here.** A directly analogous consumer-banking-app automatic-enrollment intervention with real effect sizes and evidence that even a well-targeted default leaves substantial value unrealized — useful benchmark for what to expect from opt-out round-up enrollment.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments across major UK banks.

**Limitations.**

- alerts, not a savings/contribution feature
- does not report multi-month enrollment persistence or opt-out rates specifically

## 7. Regulating Dark Patterns

Martin Brenncke · 2023 · Aston Publications Explorer (Aston University) · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2310.00340>

**Key finding.** Maps EU dark-pattern law onto a six-category taxonomy of autonomy violations, arguing that EU regulation of choice architecture is fundamentally about protecting consumer autonomy rather than banning influence per se.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C5 distributional and manipulation risk (3/3). Only shortlisted paper addressing the EU dark-pattern/manipulation-risk question the brief flags as a reputational constraint on the entry decision.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the legal test for whether pre-selecting round-up enrollment or a pre-set multiplier reads as a helpful default versus a regulable autonomy violation — directly bears on which of the three entry designs Legal's clearance actually survives reputational and regulatory scrutiny for.

**Method.** Doctrinal/legal analysis constructing a normative taxonomy from EU statutes and case law. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- legal/doctrinal analysis, not empirical evidence on enrollment or amounts
- general consumer law, not financial-product specific

## 8. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** With automatic 401(k) enrollment, participation exceeds 85% at three firms, but about 80% of participants initially accept both the low default savings rate (2-3%) and a conservative default fund, and half still do at three years -- participation gains and low-default anchoring roughly offset each other in overall asset accumulation, though the lower tail of savers benefits clearly.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The classic anchoring-at-default-level result the current literature builds on and argues with; directly informs the multiplier pre-selection question even though dated and off-domain.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration that a pre-set default level anchors most participants for years, which is the single strongest piece of evidence for choosing the multiplier level carefully rather than defaulting to whatever is administratively convenient.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms tracking 401(k) participation, default acceptance, and asset accumulation over three years.

**Limitations.**

- Employer 401(k) setting from an older era of retirement research, cited as the classic study the brief says to weight less heavily
- no online/app context

## 9. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A field experiment on a Brazilian financial-management platform found payment reminders cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised checking-account overdraft fees by 9%, with users who already overdraft seeing a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Best available consumer-fintech evidence of distributional harm from automated interventions, directly answering the brief's 'who is helped and who is hurt' question.

**Why it matters here.** Direct evidence, in a self-directed consumer fintech app, that a well-intentioned automated nudge can help most users while actively harming a vulnerable subgroup — exactly the heterogeneity risk the brief warns against building blind to.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment with a consumer fintech/financial-management platform.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrollment defaults or savings contribution levels
- Brazilian credit-card market, not EU round-up savings

## 10. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Proposes and field-tests 'enhanced active choice' — active choice with loss-framing that favors one option — as a way to get most of the automatic-enrollment benefit without a full default, explicitly recommended as a complement to or substitute for opt-out enrollment.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). The one paper offering a specific, transferable active-choice design technique rather than just comparing active choice to opt-out in the abstract.

**Why it matters here.** Supplies a concrete third design — not plain active choice but a framed variant — that the round-up feature's onboarding screen could adopt to capture more of the opt-out enrollment lift while avoiding forced-default disengagement; the specific technique (loss-framed active choice) is explicitly proposed as a savings-context substitute for automatic enrollment.

**Method.** Series of studies including two field experiments comparing standard active choice, enhanced (framed) active choice, and opt-out defaults on behavior-change outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- primary domain is medication adherence, not financial products
- abstract gives no quantified effect size for enhanced active choice itself
