## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouds the credit-card minimum-payment autopay option cuts minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but does not significantly reduce debt, because nudged users set autopay only slightly above the minimum and some drop autopay entirely, raising missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). A rigorous consumer-financial field experiment showing a default/nudge can move enrolment numbers without moving the real outcome, the core contradiction the brief asks us to test for.

**Why it matters here.** Directly supports the support team's 'vanity enrolment' worry: a design that changes nominal enrolment/behavior can leave the underlying financial outcome unchanged, which is exactly the amount-vs-enrolment distinction the round-up decision needs settled.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit card accounts, six-month follow-up, comparing behavior and debt outcomes across nudge arms.

**Limitations.**

- credit-card debt paydown, not savings accumulation
- liquidity-constrained population may not generalize to all app users

## 2. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from automatic enrolment and 0.3% from auto-escalation defaults, far smaller than headline short-run numbers, because of job turnover, withdrawal on separation, and opt-outs from escalation.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving in the medium-to-long run, directly bearing on whether opt-out enrolment numbers would be real or vanity.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of our support team's objection: the enrolment lift measured at signup shrinks dramatically once withdrawal and disengagement are tracked over time.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating employee turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out dynamics over the medium-to-long run.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated enrolment channel

## 3. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a 5-country European sample (n=4207), default risk allocations shift investment behavior by medium-to-large effect sizes, but imposing a default (especially one people refuse) significantly lowers how honest and trustworthy the advice is rated, and how a default/question is framed independently shifts the chosen allocation.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). A European, consumer-facing financial-decision study that speaks directly to both the preset-level anchoring question and the manipulative-default reputational risk the brief flags.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests the EU dark-pattern risk named in the brief: shows that a poorly-framed default can be read as manipulative and damage trust, and that framing of the default (not just its presence) anchors the chosen level.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, including a large representative multi-country European sample, manipulating default presence, framing, and refusal.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management advice context rather than a round-up savings app
- self-reported trust/rating measure, not behavioral persistence

## 4. The Effect of Choice Screens on Mobile Browser Usage: Evidence from the EU Digital Markets Act [UNVERIFIED — check manually]

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** Mandatory browser choice screens under the EU Digital Markets Act raised Firefox usage 113% on iOS and 12% on Android, with effects persisting from 15 months post-mandate onward, and platform rollout differences (new vs. existing devices) explaining cross-platform gaps.

**Method.** Difference-in-differences design around the staggered EU DMA choice-screen mandate on iOS and Android.

**Limitations.**

- browser default, not a financial savings decision
- outcome is app usage share, not enrollment/withdrawal amounts

## 5. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft alerts cut unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, worth £170-240 million market-wide, though users still captured less than half of available savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Consumer-bank, self-directed, at-scale RCT evidence on automatic enrollment into a financial nudge feature — the nearest available analogue to the round-up decision.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world precedent for opt-out enrollment into a consumer bank feature: shows a strong, quantified default effect at scale in the exact self-directed banking setting the brief cares about, and that even successful auto-enrollment leaves substantial unrealized benefit — bears directly on both entry-mode and expectations for the round-up feature.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments at major UK banks, market-wide extrapolation of savings.

**Limitations.**

- outcome is charges avoided, not direct savings-pot enrollment/engagement
- UK regulatory context rather than round-up savings specifically

## 6. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults raised campaign donations by over $43 million but also increased refund requests by almost $3 million, with the largest impact on smaller, less-experienced donors who did not adjust the amount they gave.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Consumer, self-directed digital default study with distributional harm evidence directly analogous to the dark-pattern risk named in the brief.

**Why it matters here.** A rigorous, self-directed digital-choice-architecture study showing a strong default effect that simultaneously produces detriment (refunds) concentrated on the least-experienced users — the clearest quantified precedent for the manipulation-risk trade-off the brief is weighing.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across political campaign websites that added pre-checked recurring boxes at different times.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a savings/banking product
- no multi-month persistence or withdrawal tracking

## 7. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 3/3

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrolment pushed participation above 85% across three firms, but roughly 80% of participants initially accepted both the low default savings rate (2-3%) and conservative default fund, and half still did so after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The foundational anchoring result the later literature (including several other shortlisted papers) is arguing with, directly bearing on whether a preset multiplier will anchor choices.

**Why it matters here.** The classic result establishing that a preset contribution level anchors most participants for years, the foundational evidence the anchoring literature and this project's multiplier-preset question both build on.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative data from three large firms comparing pre- and post-automatic-enrolment cohorts.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored retirement plan, pre-dates app-based liquid savings
- no year given in metadata; older/classic study relative to brief's recency preference

## 9. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A field experiment with a Brazilian financial-management platform found payment reminders cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised overdraft fees by 9%, with users who had a history of overdraft use experiencing a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Provides a rigorous, consumer-financial (non-employer) RCT showing that a nudge's average benefit conceals harm to a subgroup, the strongest available evidence on the brief's distributional/manipulation-risk question (C5).

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the brief's concern that a nudge can help most users while quietly harming a subgroup, exactly the heterogeneity risk the team must check before defaulting everyone into round-up savings; it argues for segmenting who gets defaulted in rather than assuming a uniform benefit.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment on a consumer financial-management platform in Brazil; fee outcomes tracked across credit card and checking accounts.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrolment defaults or preset contribution levels
- Brazilian credit/overdraft context rather than EU round-up savings
- no data on persistence beyond the fee-outcome window reported in the abstract
