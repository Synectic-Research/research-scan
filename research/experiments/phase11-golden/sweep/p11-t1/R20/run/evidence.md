## 1. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a 5-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges shift risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes but lower how trustworthy the advice is rated, especially when the default is refused, and framing of the default question itself (asking how much to leave safe vs. invest) shifts the outcome.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer financial experiment on default framing and perceived manipulation, the closest direct evidence for the dark-pattern risk the brief flags.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests whether a financial default reads as helpful or manipulative in a European consumer context, and shows framing of the same default changes outcomes — both feed straight into the entry-design and level-anchoring decisions.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative multi-country European survey experiment on financial advice defaults.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management risk allocation, not round-up savings specifically
- advice-rating outcome is a perception proxy, not actual retention or amount saved

## 2. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouded the minimum-payment autopay option cut minimum-only payers by 23% but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because nudged users often chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Consumer, self-directed field experiment showing a nudge succeeding on enrolment optics but failing on the real outcome the brief cares about.

**Why it matters here.** Shows that changing an enrolment default can move a headline enrolment metric while leaving the underlying financial behaviour essentially unchanged — the exact 'vanity enrolment' failure mode our support team predicts for round-up.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit cards with six-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- credit card repayment context, not savings
- six-month horizon only, not 12 months

## 3. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** In 8.6 million taxi tipping decisions, 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out effort cost together (friction complementarity); adding a lower preset option (15%) captured 69% of the achievable consumer welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest available quantitative model of how preset menu design shapes which option consumers end up choosing, directly informing the level-anchoring decision.

**Why it matters here.** A direct, quantified test of how a menu of preset options anchors choices and what happens when you add or change presets — precisely the question of whether shipping 1x/2x/5x versus a blank field will anchor round-up levels.

**Method.** Structural model estimated on transaction data plus an independent stated-preference survey; counterfactual simulations of alternative preset menus.

**Limitations.**

- tipping, not savings, so stakes and framing differ
- single point-in-time choices, no persistence or withdrawal data

## 4. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrolment and of default contribution auto-escalation; steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% (auto-enrollment) and 0.3% (auto-escalation), and only 40% of people escalate on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that defaults reliably and durably raise saving, which the brief asked us to test.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantitative statement of our support team's objection: default-driven enrolment gains shrink dramatically once turnover, withdrawal and opt-out are accounted for over time.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job turnover, withdrawal on separation, and opt-out from auto-escalation; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated auto-escalation differs from a self-set multiplier

## 5. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for when default choice architecture does or does not produce lasting ('downstream') effects on consumer behaviour, identifying time course, prior preferences, and trade-off salience as key moderators.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). The review/synthesis giving a structured account of default persistence mechanisms the brief asks for.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a framework for exactly the persistence question the brief asks — what to measure and when to expect a default's effect to fade versus stick — useful for designing the 6-12 month follow-up analysis.

**Method.** Conceptual synthesis and research agenda; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical/conceptual, not new empirical effect sizes
- general consumer behaviour, not financial products specifically

## 6. Regulating Dark Patterns

Martin Brenncke · 2023 · Aston Publications Explorer (Aston University) · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2310.00340>

**Key finding.** Maps EU laws addressing dark patterns (including pre-selecting options by default) and develops a six-category taxonomy of autonomy violations to distinguish acceptable choice-architecture influence from regulable manipulation.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C5 distributional and manipulation risk (3/3). Directly addresses the brief's stated EU dark-pattern reputational risk for the entry-design decision.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the regulatory framework for judging whether an opt-out enrolment design in an EU consumer banking app would read as a helpful default or a sanctionable dark pattern — directly shapes the legal risk assessment behind the entry-design decision.

**Method.** Doctrinal/legal analysis and taxonomy construction across EU law.

**Limitations.**

- legal/normative analysis, not behavioural effect data
- not specific to savings or fintech products

## 7. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrollment pushed participation above 85%, but roughly 80% of employees initially accepted both the low default savings rate and conservative default fund, and half still did so after three years, largely offsetting participation gains against under-saving at the default level.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The canonical anchoring-on-defaults result the newer literature argues with; included as the foundational reference point.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration that presets anchor people at the default level for years, which is the central risk of shipping a pre-selected round-up multiplier.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms comparing pre/post automatic enrollment.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) plan, not a consumer app
- classic study predating the current app/liquidity/regulatory context the brief says has since changed

## 8. Automatic Enrollment with a 12% Default Contribution Rate

John Beshears, Ruofei Guo, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31601>

**Key finding.** At a 12% default contribution rate, 25% of employees had not opted out after 12 months versus roughly 50% at lower defaults typically studied, and many remaining defaulters had contribution rates far above what their income would predict.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best available evidence that preset default levels anchor final contribution amounts and that this effect differs by income, directly informing the round-up multiplier default choice.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests whether the level of a preset default anchors people at a value they would not otherwise choose, which is exactly the round-up multiplier anchoring question, and shows anchoring can be stronger for lower-income users.

**Method.** Empirical analysis of a firm's retirement plan with an unusually high 12% default, compared against a literature review of lower-default plans.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored retirement plan, not a liquid consumer savings product
- no employer-trust-mechanism discussion
- single-firm case study

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A randomized nudge (payment reminders) reduced credit-card late fees by 14% but increased overdraft fees by 9%, concentrated among users with a history of overdraft use, so those users saw a 5% net increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). A rigorous RCT in a genuine consumer fintech setting showing distributional harm from a nudge, directly contradicting the 'defaults/nudges always help' premise and matching the brief's call for heterogeneity evidence.

**Why it matters here.** Directly demonstrates that a well-intentioned nudge in a consumer digital financial product can hurt a vulnerable subgroup even while helping most users on average — exactly the 'works on average by hurting those who did not need it' risk the brief asks about for round-up enrollment.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment on a financial management platform in Brazil, credit card and checking account fee data.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrollment defaults specifically
- Brazilian credit-card market, not EU regulatory context
- abstract doesn't report enrollment/persistence over 6-12 months
