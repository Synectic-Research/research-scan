**Method.** Conceptual/theoretical synthesis and research agenda (Consumer Psychology Review), abstract-only.

- theoretical/agenda-setting rather than a specific empirical estimate

## 2. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the autopay minimum-payment option reduced minimum-only credit card payments by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, with liquidity-constrained cardholders best explaining the null financial effect.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Closest available consumer-financial field experiment showing enrollment changes need not translate into changed financial outcomes, bearing directly on whether defaults change amount saved or only nominal enrollment.

**Why it matters here.** Shows in a real, self-directed consumer financial product that changing nominal enrollment via a default-like nudge does not translate into the intended outcome — the clearest direct evidence for the support team's 'enrolment is vanity' worry.

**Method.** Field experiment (RCT) on a credit-card autopay enrollment nudge with six-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt context, not a savings product
- the manipulation is a shrouding nudge, not a direct opt-in/opt-out/active-choice comparison

## 3. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across experiments including a representative five-European-country sample (n=4207), default nudges produced medium-to-large effects on risk-taking but also lowered ratings of the advisor's advice, especially when the default was refused, with perceived honesty mediating the reaction; framing of the default question produced smaller additional effects.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The only paper in the set giving direct EU consumer evidence on when a financial default is perceived as manipulative, bearing on reputational/dark-pattern risk.

**Why it matters here.** Gives direct EU consumer evidence that a financial default can backfire on trust/perceived honesty when overridden or refused — informing when a round-up default reads as helpful versus manipulative, the dark-pattern risk the brief flags.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative multi-country European sample, on default framing in financial risk-taking decisions.

**Limitations.**

- setting is investment/wealth-management advice with an advisor present, not a fully self-directed app
- no reported 6-12 month retention outcomes

## 4. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults on political campaign websites raised donations by over $43 million but also increased requested refunds by almost $3 million, with larger effects on smaller and less-experienced donors who did not compensate by changing donation amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest real-world evidence that recurring-financial defaults produce measurable manipulation/regret costs concentrated on the least-experienced users, directly bearing on the enrollment-vs-engagement trade-off.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the support team's concern: an opt-out-style default on a recurring financial commitment lifts nominal sign-ups but generates disproportionate regret/refunds among smaller, less experienced users — a real-money analog to complaint risk from passive enrollment.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across campaign websites around the 2020 U.S. election.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a savings product
- no data on long-term retention beyond refund requests

## 5. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics shrink automatic enrollment and auto-escalation effects: steady-state savings rates rise by only 0.6% of income from automatic enrollment and 0.3% from default auto-escalation, and only 40% of participants escalate on the first scheduled date, with more opting out over time.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest quantified contradiction of the 'defaults always help' premise, and the closest prior work on medium/long-run attenuation of default effects.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the premise that defaults reliably lift saving in the medium/long run once turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out are accounted for — exactly the support team's objection, with quantified effect sizes.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job separation, vesting, and opt-out dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- abstract-only, no consumer fintech context

## 7. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts at UK banks reduced unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, worth an estimated £170-240 million market-wide, though alerts captured less than half of potential savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly analogous consumer-banking opt-out feature with quantified benefit, the strongest same-setting evidence in the shortlist.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real analog we have: an opt-out (automatic enrollment) consumer-banking-app feature with measured financial outcomes, giving a benchmark effect size and showing defaults only partially close the potential-savings gap.

**Method.** At-scale field experiments run by major U.K. banks (RCT-style rollout).

**Limitations.**

- outcome is charge avoidance via alerts, not a savings-pot product
- does not report multi-year retention or disengagement specifically

## 8. Default Options and Retirement Saving Dynamics

Taha Choukhmane · 2025 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210881>

**Key finding.** Auto-enrollment gains in participation and contributions, replicated at 12 months, attenuate substantially by 36 months, with modest average savings increases at the longer horizon but a significant reduction in savings inequality; a life-cycle model fits this with an estimated switching cost of about $250.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest prior quantified evidence on whether default-driven enrolment gains persist at 12 vs. 36 months, the persistence horizon the brief specifically asks about.

**Why it matters here.** Directly quantifies the attenuation of default effects between 12 and 36 months, giving a concrete benchmark for what persistence looks like and by how much enrolment gains erode — the exact horizon the brief asks about.

**Method.** Analysis of over 100 US retirement plans plus a representative UK panel, combined with a structural life-cycle consumption-savings model.

**Limitations.**

- retirement savings plans (US/UK), not a liquid consumer savings app
- switching-cost estimate depends on structural model assumptions

## 9. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Using 41 million Danish savings records, automatic employer contributions substantially raised total wealth accumulation, while subsidies requiring active choice induced only about 1 cent of extra saving per dollar of subsidy, because roughly 85% of individuals are 'passive savers' unresponsive to subsidies but moved by automatic contributions.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Population-scale causal evidence that passive defaults move actual saved amounts while active elective incentives barely do — directly answers the brief's 'amount saved vs. nominal enrollment' question.

**Why it matters here.** The strongest available evidence that defaults change actual savings amounts, not just nominal enrollment, and that most people don't respond to elective incentives — a central input to whether enrollment should default users in versus rely on active-choice prompts.

**Method.** Population-scale administrative panel data from Denmark exploiting variation in subsidy and automatic-contribution policies; decomposes 'active' vs 'passive' saver responses.

**Limitations.**

- Danish retirement-savings accounts, not a liquid consumer banking app
- government subsidy vs. automatic employer contribution, not opt-in/opt-out/active-choice as this brief defines them
- does not test active choice as a distinct enrollment mechanism

## 10. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** Across three large firms, automatic 401(k) enrollment pushed participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants initially accepted both the low default contribution rate and conservative default fund, and half were still at the default rate and fund after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The strongest available long-horizon (3-year) evidence that a preset default level anchors contribution choices and offsets participation gains — the closest prior work on the Level decision.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the clearest quantified precedent for the anchoring worry — a preset default level doesn't just get accepted at signup, it persists for years — directly informing whether a pre-selected round-up multiplier will anchor most users long-term, and shows the participation-vs-amount tradeoff.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative-data analysis of automatic-enrollment 401(k) programs at three large US firms.

**Limitations.**

- Employer-sponsored 401(k) plans, not a self-directed consumer savings feature
- 2001-era data predating current fintech/regulatory norms
- does not test active choice as a third option
