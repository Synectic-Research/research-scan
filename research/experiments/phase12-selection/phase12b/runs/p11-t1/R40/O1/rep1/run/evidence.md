## 1. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a 4,207-person study spanning five European countries, defaults produced medium-to-large effects on risk-taking, but also lowered ratings of the advisor's advice, especially when the targeted default was refused — with framing of the default question shifting outcomes by smaller amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU-based, consumer self-directed financial-decision evidence on when a default reads as helpful versus manipulative, directly informing the reputational-risk constraint in the brief.

**Why it matters here.** Directly answers the EU dark-pattern question in the brief: defaults move behavior but can damage perceived trustworthiness of the institution, especially when a poorly-fitted default is refused — a real reputational-risk signal for choosing opt-out enrollment or a mismatched preset multiplier.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative multi-country European sample, on financial risk-taking decisions with wealth-manager defaults.

**Limitations.**

- single-session experimental measure, no 6-12 month persistence data
- wealth-management risk allocation rather than round-up savings specifically

## 2. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouded the credit-card minimum-payment autopay option cut minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce debt, partly because it also reduced autopay enrollment and increased missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Shows a default/nudge changing nominal enrollment behavior while failing to change the substantive financial outcome, in a genuinely consumer self-directed financial product.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests the brief's central worry: a choice-architecture change can move the enrollment/behavior number without moving the outcome that matters, and can create new harm (missed payments) for liquidity-constrained users — the clearest self-directed consumer-financial analogue we have.

**Method.** Field experiment on real credit-card accounts with six-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- credit-card debt repayment, not a savings feature
- shrouding a default is a stronger intervention than the opt-in/opt-out/active-choice menu under consideration

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from automatic enrollment and 0.3% from default auto-escalation once job turnover, balance withdrawal at separation, and opt-out from escalation are accounted for; only 40% of auto-escalated employees escalate on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that defaults durably raise saving amounts, using the most rigorous long-run design available.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of the support team's objection: default-driven enrollment gains are much smaller in steady state than the headline participation-rate effect suggests.

**Method.** Longitudinal administrative analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job separation and withdrawal dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-plan turnover dynamics (vesting, job separation) don't map onto a banking app

## 4. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults on political campaign websites increased donations by over $43 million but also increased refund requests by almost $3 million, with larger effects on smaller and less-experienced donors who did not compensate by changing donation amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the simple defaults-always-help premise by documenting donor regret/refunds and disproportionate impact on inexperienced, small contributors from a recurring financial default.

**Why it matters here.** A rare study of a recurring-financial-commitment default in a self-directed consumer setting, showing such defaults disproportionately affect naive and small users and generate regret (refunds) — directly relevant to the dark-pattern and vulnerable-user risk of defaulting people into ongoing round-up saving.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across campaign websites' default rollout timing, 2020 US election.

**Limitations.**

- Political donations, not a savings or banking product
- US setting rather than EU consumer-protection regulatory context

## 5. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** Structural analysis of 8.6 million tipping decisions finds 84% of default adherence comes from the interaction of deviation cost and opt-out cost together; adding a lower option to a fixed default menu (a 15% tip below 20/25/30%) captures 69% of achievable consumer welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Provides a directly transferable structural-model method for reasoning about preset-menu design (which default to pick, what happens if you add another option) — the mechanism explicitly named as analogous to the round-up multiplier decision.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a transferable method and finding for the level-preset decision: a menu of preset multipliers with one selected functions like the tip-menu structure studied here, and shows how adding/adjusting the menu of preset options shifts welfare and adherence — directly informs whether to add a lower multiplier option and where to anchor it.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on transaction data, validated against an independent stated-preference survey.

**Limitations.**

- taxi tipping, not a savings product
- consumer preferences for tipping norms may not generalize to savings-rate preferences

## 6. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for when default effects persist or fade downstream, driven by the time course of choice/consumption, prior preferences, and salience of trade-offs.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). General consumer-behavior review addressing exactly the persistence-of-defaults question the brief asks about, in a non-employer setting.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the team a structured way to predict and measure whether the round-up default's effect on enrollment will persist or decay by 6-12 months, and what moderators (salience, prior intent) to watch for.

**Method.** Conceptual synthesis and framework-building across the consumer-defaults literature. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical/synthesis paper, no new effect-size estimates
- not specific to financial products

## 7. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** Automatic (opt-out) enrollment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts at UK banks reduced unarranged overdraft charges by 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges by 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in market-wide annual savings, though alerts captured less than half of consumers' potential savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest real-world consumer-banking analogue to opt-out enrollment into a beneficial feature, quantifying the scale of the default effect.

**Why it matters here.** Demonstrates that opt-out default enrollment into a protective banking feature produces large, real, at-scale behavioral effects in the same UK consumer-banking context as our app, directly supporting opt-out as a viable entry design.

**Method.** At-scale field experiments at major UK banks.

**Limitations.**

- Feature is a warning alert rather than a savings-contribution decision with a preset level
- Does not report 6-12 month persistence, withdrawal, or complaint outcomes

## 8. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In an RCT of behavioral nudges for voluntary pension saving in China, all tested nudges — including default options — significantly reduced willingness to contribute relative to control, with heterogeneous backfire effects by income, education, and gender.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). One of the few studies here finding default options backfire rather than help, directly answering the brief's request for evidence contradicting the defaults-always-help premise.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the brief's working assumption that defaults reliably raise participation, showing default-option nudges can backfire and reduce contribution willingness for specific demographic segments — a strong caution before defaulting everyone in.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey, n=220.

**Limitations.**

- Voluntary pension saving survey in China, cultural/institutional context differs from EU consumer banking
- Small sample (n=220) and stated intent rather than observed behavior

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** Payment reminders reduced credit card late-payment fees by 14% but increased overdraft fees by 9%, with users who had a history of overdraft use experiencing a net 5% increase in total fees while other users saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Strong evidence on heterogeneous/distributional effects of a financial nudge and a clear contradiction of the 'nudges/defaults always help' premise, in a self-directed consumer financial app setting.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the brief's concern about who is helped and who is hurt: a well-intentioned nudge in a consumer fintech setting produced a subgroup that was made worse off, which is exactly the distributional risk the round-up enrolment decision needs to guard against before defaulting everyone in.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment with a financial management platform in Brazil, studying direct and indirect effects of nudging on fee outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrolment defaults or contribution-level anchoring
- Brazilian credit/overdraft context rather than EU round-up savings
- no measurement of persistence at 6-12 months, only aggregate fee outcomes
- no active-choice comparison arm
