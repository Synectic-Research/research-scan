## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the minimum-payment autopay option cut minimum-only payers by 23% at 6 months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because nudged cardholders picked autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum and liquidity-constrained cardholders simply missed more payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Consumer financial nudge study showing enrolment-metric movement without behavioural-outcome movement, the core trade-off the brief asks about.

**Why it matters here.** Directly undercuts the 'defaults raise enrolment therefore they help' logic in a consumer financial product: a choice-architecture nudge changed the nominal enrolment metric without changing the underlying financial outcome, exactly the support team's worry about vanity enrolment.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit cards with 6-month follow-up, large administrative dataset.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not savings
- shrouding is a different mechanism than opt-out/opt-in enrolment

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across experiments including a representative 5-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges shift risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes but also lower ratings of the advisor's advice, especially when the default is refused, with perceived wealth-manager honesty mediating the effect; framing of the default presentation (asking how much to invest vs. leave safe; accept vs. change) produces smaller but real anchoring effects.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer financial-decision experiment on default and framing effects on chosen levels and perceived manipulation, squarely on both open design decisions.

**Why it matters here.** Directly informs both open decisions: it shows a preset default can anchor the chosen amount and that a rejected/individualized default carries a reputational cost, i.e., evidence on exactly the 'helpful vs manipulative default' line that matters for an EU consumer product.

**Method.** Multiple experiments, including a large representative multi-country European survey experiment.

**Limitations.**

- financial-advice/wealth-management framing rather than a round-up savings feature
- outcome is stated risk allocation, not observed behaviour over time

## 3. The Behavioral Foundations of Default Effects: Theory and Evidence from Medicare Part D

Zarek Brot-Goldberg, Timothy Layton, Boris Vabson, Adelina Yanyue Wang · 2023 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210013>

**Key finding.** Using three natural experiments, default rules in Medicare Part D have large, persistent effects on enrolment and drug utilization, and beneficiary passivity is extremely insensitive even when the default causes substantial losses; variation in active choice tracks transitory attention shocks rather than stable attentiveness.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest available individual (non-employer) choice-architecture evidence on persistence and insensitivity of default effects, which the brief needs precisely because it wants defaults studied outside the employer-trust mechanism.

**Why it matters here.** Strongest available evidence that default effects can persist and be highly insensitive to outcome quality for individuals choosing for themselves (not via an employer), directly bearing on whether an opt-out round-up default will 'stick' regardless of fit, and on whether active-choice variation is meaningful signal or noise.

**Method.** Natural-experiment / random-assignment design across three settings, individual-level administrative data.

**Limitations.**

- health-insurance/drug-plan setting, not consumer savings
- low-income Medicare population differs from banking-app users

## 4. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics (job separation, withdrawal on separation, opt-out of auto-escalation) shrink the effect of automatic enrolment and auto-escalation to a steady-state savings-rate increase of only 0.6% and 0.3% of income respectively, with only 40% of auto-escalation defaults sticking at the first escalation date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that default enrolment durably raises savings amounts, with concrete effect sizes.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of the support team's objection: default-driven enrolment gains erode substantially once realistic attrition and opt-out dynamics are modeled, which is exactly the persistence question the brief needs answered before defaulting everyone in.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans, abstract-only for full methods.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- no data on a round-up-style consumer product

## 5. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model on 8.6 million taxi tipping decisions finds default adherence arises from complementary deviation and opt-out costs (84% of adherence needs both frictions together), preferences are stable across default changes, and adding an intermediate default tip option would capture 69% of the achievable welfare gain at 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Large-scale consumer digital-payment study quantifying exactly how and why preset default levels anchor choices, directly bearing on the multiplier-preset decision.

**Why it matters here.** Directly informs the round-up multiplier design: it shows a well-chosen intermediate default level can capture most of the achievable welfare gain, and that default power comes from friction (effort to change) more than preference-shifting, which bears on whether a preset 1x/2x/5x default will anchor people away from their true preference.

**Method.** Structural econometric model plus stated-preference survey, large-scale transaction data (8.6M decisions).

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions, not a savings product
- single-transaction choice, no persistence/withdrawal data over months

## 6. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In a 220-participant RCT, social-norm, framing, default-option, and positive-narrative nudges all significantly reduced willingness to contribute to voluntary pension savings in China relative to control, with default options and positive narratives producing the largest backfire effects and impacts varying by income, education, and gender.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest contradicting evidence in the shortlist that default options can backfire and reduce contribution willingness.

**Why it matters here.** Direct contradicting evidence against the working assumption that a default lifts enrollment: in this self-directed voluntary-savings RCT the default option actually reduced willingness to contribute, exactly the risk the support team raises, with heterogeneity across demographic groups.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey (n=220) testing behavioral nudges, including a default option, on voluntary pension contribution decisions.

**Limitations.**

- hypothetical online survey rather than a live financial product
- conducted in China, a different cultural/regulatory context than the EU
- voluntary pension framing rather than a liquid round-up savings pot

## 8. Employer-Based Short-Term Savings Accounts

Sarah Holmes Berk, John Beshears, Jay Garg, James J. Choi et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32074>

**Key finding.** An opt-in payroll short-term (liquid, withdrawable-any-time) savings account at UK firms saw only 0.7% of eligible employees ever activate it, but of those who did, 96% still had a balance and 87% still received automatic contributions after 12 months.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Structurally the nearest product match to the round-up feature (liquid, withdrawable, opt-in savings) despite the employer channel, giving directly comparable take-up/retention numbers.

**Why it matters here.** The closest existing product analog to the round-up feature (short-term, liquid, withdrawable savings) shows opt-in produces very high retention among the self-selected but vanishingly low take-up overall -- the exact trade-off the brief is weighing between opt-in reach and engagement quality.

**Method.** Field study of a UK employer payroll savings program, administrative account data over 12 months.

**Limitations.**

- delivered through an employer payroll channel, so the 'trusted intermediary' effect the brief says doesn't transfer may inflate retention
- not a consumer banking app

## 9. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Using 41 million Danish observations, automatic employer contributions substantially raised total wealth accumulation, while active-choice tax subsidies induced only 1 cent of extra saving per $1 of subsidy, because 85% of people are 'passive savers' unresponsive to subsidies but heavily influenced by automatic contributions, while the wealthier 15% of 'active savers' mainly shift assets across accounts.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Provides the largest-scale causal estimate distinguishing whether automatic mechanisms change real saved amounts versus active-choice mechanisms merely reshuffling assets — the strongest-evidence answer to the brief's central amount-vs-enrolment question, even though the setting is retirement accounts.

**Why it matters here.** The strongest available causal evidence that defaults change actual saved amounts for the majority who would otherwise not act, while active-choice incentives mostly relabel existing savings — directly bearing on whether the round-up default changes real saving or just nominal enrolment, and on who is helped versus not.

**Method.** Administrative panel analysis of the entire Danish population's savings accounts (41 million observations) comparing responses to active-choice tax subsidies versus automatic employer contributions.

**Limitations.**

- retirement/tax-subsidized accounts embedded in the Danish welfare/tax system, not a self-directed liquid consumer app
- government/employer-mediated contributions rather than a bank round-up feature
- no measure of user-perceived manipulation or complaint behavior
