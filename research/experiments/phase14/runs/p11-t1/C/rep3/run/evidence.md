## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** Shrouding the exact-minimum autopay option cut minimum-only credit card payments by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce debt, and reduced autopay enrollment enough to increase missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Directly tests whether a default/nudge changes actual financial behavior versus just nominal enrollment status, the core trade-off the brief asks about.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a default/nudge can shift nominal sign-up behavior substantially while leaving the underlying financial outcome unchanged or worse, directly supporting the support team's concern that enrolment numbers can be a vanity metric for round-ups.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit card autopay defaults with a six-month follow-up window (NBER working paper).

**Limitations.**

- credit-card debt paydown, not a savings product
- no measure of persistence beyond 6 months
- liquidity constraints identified as the mechanism, may not generalize to all round-up users

## 2. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and conceptual framework for downstream default effects, identifying time course of choice, antecedent preferences, and trade-off salience as moderators of whether default effects persist or backfire.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Directly addresses persistence and downstream consequences of default choice architecture in consumer settings, the central open question in the brief.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a structured way to predict when a round-up default will persist versus produce quick disengagement, directly informing what to measure post-launch at 6-12 months.

**Method.** Conceptual review and framework synthesis of consumer default-effect literature; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical/conceptual, not new empirical data
- not specific to financial products
- no effect sizes given

## 3. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across experiments including a five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges shift risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes, but applying (or refusing) a default lowers the client's rating of the advisor's advice, mediated by perceived honesty, and framing of the default question shifts the chosen allocation.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). EU consumer financial experiment quantifying both anchoring on default framing and perceived manipulation risk, matching two of the brief's key open questions directly.

**Why it matters here.** Directly measures when a default reads as manipulative in a consumer financial EU context and shows framing effects on the chosen level, bearing on both the entry-design manipulation risk and the multiplier anchoring question.

**Method.** Series of controlled experiments, including a large representative five-country European sample, on default and framing effects in financial risk-allocation decisions.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management advisory context rather than a banking app round-up feature
- single risk-allocation decision, no long-term persistence data
- no direct opt-in/opt-out enrollment comparison

## 4. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Steady-state saving rates rise by only 0.6% of income from automatic enrollment and 0.3% from default auto-escalation once turnover, pre-vesting withdrawals, and opt-outs are accounted for; only 40% of auto-escalated employees stay on schedule at the first escalation date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving with a quantified estimate, satisfying the brief's request for contradicting evidence.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of the counter-argument: measured default effects on enrollment substantially overstate real long-run savings impact once attrition and opt-out are included.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans' administrative data incorporating employee turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- employer-administered rather than self-directed app
- does not address active choice or manipulation perception

## 5. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft alerts cuts unarranged overdraft charges 17–19% and arranged overdraft charges 4–8%, an estimated £170–240 million in annual market-wide savings, yet consumers still capture less than half of the potential savings available.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Same setting as the brief — consumer bank app, opt-out enrollment into a financial feature — with real behavioral and monetary outcomes, the strongest direct precedent available.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world evidence of opt-out-style enrollment into a bank feature producing measurable financial benefit at scale, while showing defaults alone leave substantial value unrealized — directly relevant to whether opt-out enrollment into round-up savings changes real outcomes, not just nominal sign-up.

**Method.** Large-scale field experiments (automatic enrollment vs. control) run at major UK banks, with market-wide extrapolation.

**Limitations.**

- overdraft alerts are a notification mechanism, not a savings sweep
- no data on contribution-level anchoring

## 6. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults on political campaign websites increased donations by over $43 million but also increased requested refunds by almost $3 million, with the largest impact on smaller, default-inexperienced donors who did not compensate by changing donation amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest quantified evidence of a pre-checked financial default causing both large uptake and measurable buyer's-remorse/refund harm, directly bearing on the dark-pattern and disengagement risk the brief asks about.

**Why it matters here.** A directly analogous real-money default (a pre-checked recurring financial commitment) that both lifts nominal sign-up and generates a refund/complaint pattern mirroring the support team's fear about passively-enrolled, unhappy users, while showing the default changed how money flowed without people compensating on amount.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across U.S. political campaign websites that added pre-checked recurring-donation boxes at different times.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a savings/banking product
- no 6-12 month persistence data beyond the donation window

## 7. Default Options and Retirement Saving Dynamics

Taha Choukhmane · 2025 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210881>

**Key finding.** Auto-enrollment's participation and contribution gains, replicated at 12 months, attenuate substantially by 36 months, with modest savings increases at the longer horizon though inequality in savings is reduced; a life-cycle model estimates a $250 switching cost.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best quantified evidence on persistence/attenuation of default effects over time, the exact horizon the brief asks about.

**Why it matters here.** Best quantified evidence that default-driven enrollment gains shrink substantially at medium-run horizons, directly bearing on whether the round-up feature's enrollment lift would persist at 6-12 months.

**Method.** Analysis of over 100 US retirement plans plus a UK representative panel comparing 12-month and 36-month outcomes, with a structural life-cycle model.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plan setting rather than liquid consumer savings
- switching costs estimated for pension context may not transfer to a low-friction app toggle
- no employer-vs-app comparison offered

## 8. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model of 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions finds 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out effort cost; adding a 15% tip option below the existing 20-25-30% menu captures 69% of achievable consumer welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Provides a rigorous, transferable method for decomposing default adherence into preference vs. friction and quantifying menu-design welfare tradeoffs, directly bearing on the preset-multiplier decision.

**Why it matters here.** Quantifies how much preset-menu adherence is friction rather than preference and shows how adjusting the menu of preset options trades welfare against revenue—directly analogous to choosing which round-up multiplier to preset and whether to add options.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on large-scale transactional tipping data plus an independent stated-preference survey identifying true preferences.

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions, not savings behavior
- single transactional context (taxi payment), not a banking app
- welfare/revenue tradeoff numbers are domain-specific to tipping norms

## 9. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Using 41 million observations in Denmark, automatic (passive) employer contributions raised real wealth accumulation, while subsidies requiring active choice mostly just shifted assets between accounts; each $1 of subsidy raised total saving by only about 1 cent, and roughly 85% of individuals were 'passive savers' unresponsive to subsidies.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The strongest available quantitative evidence on whether default/passive enrolment changes real saved amounts versus just reallocating them, directly bearing on the brief's amount-vs-nominal-enrolment question and the active-choice trade-off.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a large-sample, quantified answer to whether a default changes real amounts saved or just nominal enrolment; passive defaults produced genuine new savings while active-choice-style incentives mostly moved money around, arguing against assuming active choice alone captures the benefit.

**Method.** Large-scale administrative panel data analysis of the entire Danish population's savings responses to active-choice subsidies versus passive automatic-contribution policies.

**Limitations.**

- Retirement/tax-advantaged accounts rather than a liquid consumer round-up product
- Danish population and tax system, not an app context
- No direct measurement of user-level withdrawal or disengagement over weeks/months

## 10. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** At three large firms, automatic 401(k) enrollment raised participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants initially accepted both the low default contribution rate and default conservative fund, and half still held the default rate and fund after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest direct precedent for anchoring on a pre-set contribution level, with multi-year persistence data, exactly the second design decision (preset multiplier vs blank field) the brief is deciding.

**Why it matters here.** Directly answers whether a pre-set level anchors the choice people end up making, since half of participants were still anchored on the default years later, exactly the risk in shipping a pre-selected round-up multiplier rather than a blank field.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative panel data from three large U.S. firms comparing 401(k) contribution and investment choices before/after automatic enrollment.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) employer retirement plan, the mechanism the brief says does not carry over
- Predates app-based, liquid, self-directed consumer products
- Persistence measured at 3 years for retirement contributions, not weeks/months of liquid round-up saving
