## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field-experiment nudge that shrouds the minimum-payment autopay option cuts minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but does not reduce credit card debt, and actually raises missed payments by discouraging autopay enrollment among liquidity-constrained cardholders.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Consumer self-directed financial nudge study showing enrollment/behavior change without matching real-outcome improvement, directly bearing on whether defaults would be a vanity metric here.

**Why it matters here.** Directly supports the support team's worry: a choice-architecture change can shift nominal enrollment/behavior metrics while leaving the real outcome (debt/savings) unchanged or worse, which is exactly the 'vanity enrollment number' risk for the round-up feature.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit cards with 6-month follow-up; JEL-coded econometric analysis of payment behavior.

**Limitations.**

- credit-card debt repayment context, not a savings product
- no direct test of a savings round-up mechanism
- liquidity constraints specific to indebted cardholders may not generalize to savers

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a representative five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges shift investment risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes, but lower ratings of advice quality compared to no default, especially when the targeted default is refused; framing of the default question (how much to invest vs. how much to leave safe) also shifts the amount allocated to the risky option.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer-financial experiment on default framing effects on both behavior and perceived manipulation, hitting nearly every sub-criterion the brief cares about.

**Why it matters here.** Directly addresses the EU dark-pattern reputational risk of defaults in financial products, and shows preset framing anchors the invested/allocated amount — bearing on both the entry-mechanism and preset-level decisions.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, including a large representative multi-country European sample, in a wealth-management/investment-advice context.

**Limitations.**

- investment risk-taking with a wealth manager, not a round-up savings multiplier specifically
- experimental/survey setting rather than a live product deployment

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrollment and default auto-escalation: steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of auto-escalation defaults are followed at the first escalation date, declining further later.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving durably, which the brief explicitly asked us to look for.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest statement of our support team's objection: the enrolment lift is real at signup and much smaller later, and preset escalation is not durably followed — a direct caution against treating opt-out enrollment counts as the success metric.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating employee turnover, withdrawal upon separation, and auto-escalation opt-out, abstract-only for further detail.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- abstract gives limited detail on mechanisms behind opt-out of escalation

## 4. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and conceptual framework for when defaults do or do not produce lasting ('downstream') behavior change, keyed to time course of choice/consumption, antecedent preferences, and salience of trade-offs.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Directly targets the brief's central question of whether default effects on enrollment persist into real downstream behavior, with a framework for measurement.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a framework for predicting and measuring whether the round-up default will produce genuine lasting engagement versus a superficial enrollment bump, directly shaping what to track at 6-12 months.

**Method.** Theoretical synthesis and taxonomy-building drawing on the existing default-effects literature; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- conceptual/theoretical, no new empirical effect sizes
- general consumer behavior framework, not specific to financial savings products

## 5. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts reduced unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in annual market-wide savings, though enrolled consumers still captured less than half of available savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Same setting as the brief (UK consumer bank, self-directed customers) and directly tests automatic enrollment's real effect size and gap versus potential benefit.

**Why it matters here.** Direct evidence that a bank can auto-enroll consumers into a low-friction financial feature with measurable real-world benefit, but also that auto-enrollment alone leaves much potential benefit unrealized, cautioning against treating enrollment numbers as the full picture.

**Method.** Large-scale field experiments at major UK banks testing automatic enrollment into SMS alerts.

**Limitations.**

- Alerts, not a savings-contribution product, so amount-saved dynamics may differ
- No multi-year retention or withdrawal data reported

## 6. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults on political campaign websites increased donations by over $43 million but also increased requested refunds by almost $3 million, with the largest effects on smaller, less-experienced donors who likely did not intend recurring commitments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Demonstrates the mechanism the brief calls a dark default, a pre-checked recurring financial commitment, causing complaint-like behavior disproportionately among vulnerable users; the recurring-transaction default is an explicit method transfer to the round-up default question.

**Why it matters here.** Direct real-world evidence that a pre-checked recurring-transaction default, structurally identical to auto-enrolling users into an ongoing round-up commitment, drives unintended enrollment and refund/complaint-like behavior concentrated on the least-experienced users, exactly the support team's disengagement/complaint concern.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences natural experiment across U.S. political campaign websites' donation checkout pages.

**Limitations.**

- Political donations, not a savings product, though the recurring-default mechanism directly transfers
- No 6+ month retention data beyond refund requests

## 7. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In a 220-participant RCT, all tested behavioral nudges, including default options, significantly reduced willingness to contribute to voluntary pension savings in China, with default-option and positive-narrative nudges producing the largest backfire and effects varying by income, education, and gender.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The explicit contradicting evidence the brief asked for: a controlled experiment where default nudges reduced rather than increased savings intent.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the brief's working assumption that defaults reliably raise enrollment/contribution, showing defaults can backfire in a self-directed savings context with highly heterogeneous effects by demographic group, exactly the caution the brief asks for.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey testing social-norm, framing, default-option, and narrative nudges on pension contribution decisions.

**Limitations.**

- Chinese cultural/institutional context, not EU
- Online survey RCT measuring stated willingness, not real enrollment or 6-12 month persistence in an actual product

## 9. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A field-experiment reminder nudge cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised overdraft fees by 9%, with the harm concentrated in users with an overdraft history who saw a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Provides rigorous, consumer-financial-app RCT evidence of exactly the distributional harm-to-a-subgroup risk the brief flags as a reason not to build something that only 'works on average'.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the brief's concern that a nudge/default which helps on average can systematically harm a vulnerable subgroup, making the case for measuring heterogeneous outcomes (e.g. overdraft-prone or low-balance users) rather than just aggregate enrolment or savings lift before deciding on opt-out enrolment.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment with a financial management platform in Brazil, using administrative fee data across credit card and checking accounts.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders rather than enrolment defaults or contribution-level anchoring
- Brazilian credit/overdraft market context, not EU round-up savings
- no explicit 6-12 month persistence tracking of the intervention's effect

## 10. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Field experiments show that 'active choice' (forcing a decision, no default) and a novel 'enhanced active choice' design (highlighting the losses of the non-preferred option) can motivate behavior change as an alternative to opt-out defaults, especially where automatic enrolment is infeasible or ethically fraught.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). The key paper directly proposing and testing the active-choice design alternative named in the brief's entry decision, with an explicit note that its logic was built for savings/opt-out contexts.

**Why it matters here.** Introduces and field-tests the exact 'active choice' entry mechanism the brief is weighing, plus an 'enhanced active choice' variant that could capture much of a default's enrolment lift without the disengagement cost of opt-out.

**Method.** Series of studies including two field experiments testing active choice and enhanced active choice against control conditions, building explicitly on the active-choice enrolment literature (Carroll, Choi, Laibson, Madrian, Metrick 2009).

**Limitations.**

- evidence base is medication adherence, not financial products
- no measurement of contribution-level anchoring or long-run amount saved
- the loss-framing nudge in enhanced active choice itself risks a manipulative/dark-pattern read
