## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field-experiment nudge that discourages minimum-only credit-card payments cut minimum-only paying by 23% at six months but did not significantly reduce debt, because nudged cardholders mostly chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum and liquidity-constrained cardholders were disproportionately affected.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Consumer financial product field RCT showing enrollment-metric change without real behavioral change, with a liquidity-driven distributional finding.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the support team's objection in a real consumer financial product: a nudge changed a nominal enrollment-like metric while leaving the real outcome (debt/savings) unmoved, and hurt liquidity-constrained users most \u2014 exactly the enrollment-vs-amount and distributional questions the brief asks about.

**Method.** Field experiment with credit card issuer, six-month follow-up on payment and debt outcomes; abstract gives effect sizes.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not a savings product
- six-month horizon only
- US regulatory context, not EU

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a representative five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges on risk-taking have medium-to-large effects, but implementing a default nudge \u2014 and especially having individuals refuse it \u2014 lowers users' rating of the advice/advisor, and framing of the default presentation shifts chosen allocations.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Recent EU-sample experimental evidence directly on when a financial default reads as manipulative versus helpful, and on framing-driven anchoring \u2014 exactly the manipulation-risk and anchoring questions in the brief.

**Why it matters here.** Gives EU-context evidence that a financial default can be perceived as manipulative and damage trust when refused, directly bearing on the dark-pattern/reputational risk question, and shows framing of the default choice anchors the amount chosen \u2014 relevant to how to phrase the multiplier default.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, including a large representative multi-country EU sample, manipulating default presence and framing of a financial-allocation decision.

**Limitations.**

- risk-allocation/investment decision rather than round-up savings specifically
- wealth-manager advice framing, not a self-serve app flow
- effect on trust measured via ratings, not actual complaints or churn

## 3. The Effect of Choice Screens on Mobile Browser Usage: Evidence from the EU Digital Markets Act [UNVERIFIED — check manually]

Jesper Akesson, Kush Amlani, Raul Cepeda Suarez, Emily Chissell et al. · 2026 · Social Science Research Network · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** Following the EU Digital Markets Act's mandated browser choice screens, Firefox usage rose 113% on iOS and 12% on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual, with the effect persisting at least 15 months post-rollout.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best available recent EU evidence quantifying an active-choice mandate's effect on real behaviour, with persistence beyond a year.

**Why it matters here.** The most directly comparable EU evidence on whether an active-choice screen can substantially shift behaviour away from an incumbent default, with the effect measured well past initial rollout — central to the active-choice-vs-default decision.

**Method.** Difference-in-differences design around the staggered EU DMA choice-screen mandate on iOS and Android.

**Limitations.**

- studies browser choice, not financial contribution decisions
- cross-platform differences (iOS vs Android rollout) complicate a single effect-size estimate

## 4. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrollment and default savings-rate auto-escalation: steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of those defaulted into escalation actually escalate on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving once medium/long-run attrition is accounted for, which the brief explicitly asked us to look for.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest statement of our support team's objection: the enrollment lift is real at signup and much smaller once job turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out are accounted for, meaning enrollment numbers can be a vanity metric relative to actual amounts saved.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating turnover, vesting, withdrawal-on-separation, and opt-out from auto-escalation.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated setting
- US 401(k) institutional specifics may not map onto app defaults

## 5. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults on political campaign sites raised donations by over $43 million but also increased refund requests by almost $3 million, with the largest impact on small, first-time donors who likely did not intend the recurring commitment.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest available quantitative case of a recurring-contribution default causing disproportionate harm to inexperienced, small-scale users.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a real quantified case of a recurring-contribution default producing unintended enrolment and elevated 'refunds' (a direct analogue to complaints/withdrawals) concentrated among naive users — precisely the support team's worry, with dollar figures attached.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across multiple 2020 U.S. campaign websites exploiting different rollout timing of pre-checked recurring-donation boxes.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a banking savings product
- no 6–12 month persistence tracking beyond refund requests

## 7. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrolment into just-in-time overdraft alerts cuts unarranged overdraft charges 17–19% and arranged charges 4–8%, worth £170–240 million market-wide, though users still capture less than half of available savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly relevant, same-sector (UK banking), automatic-enrolment field evidence with quantified financial outcomes.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world analogue to our feature: a consumer bank testing automatic enrolment into a low-friction financial nudge at scale, with real behavioural and monetary effect sizes — sets the benchmark our own default-effect measurement should be compared against.

**Method.** Large-scale field experiments at major UK banks testing automatic enrolment into SMS alerts.

**Limitations.**

- studies alerts, not a savings/contribution feature per se
- no explicit 6–12 month persistence analysis reported

## 8. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In a 220-participant RCT on voluntary pension saving in China, all tested nudges — including default options — significantly reduced willingness to contribute relative to control, with the largest backfire from positive-narrative and default-option nudges, and effects varying by income, education, and gender.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest counter-evidence in this batch that a default can reduce rather than raise savings intentions, fulfilling the brief's explicit call for a contradicting study.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the 'defaults always help' premise the brief asks us to test, with an RCT rather than an anecdote, and shows the effect can reverse depending on cultural/institutional trust — exactly the kind of evidence needed before defaulting everyone in.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey testing social-norm, framing, default-option, and narrative nudges on voluntary pension contribution decisions.

**Limitations.**

- hypothetical/survey-based willingness rather than real enrolment behaviour
- Chinese voluntary-pension context, not an EU consumer savings app
- small sample (n=220)

## 10. Why Do Defaults Affect Behavior? Experimental Evidence from Afghanistan

Joshua Blumenstock, Michael Callen, Tarek Ghani · 2018 · American Economic Review · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20171676>

**Key finding.** In a randomized experiment on salary-linked savings accounts in Afghanistan, default enrollment increased participation by 40 percentage points — equivalent to a 50% matching incentive — driven mainly by present-biased preferences and the cognitive cost of evaluating savings scenarios, and it also shifted attitudes toward saving.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Rare experimental evidence quantifying the default effect size and its causal mechanism, directly bearing on whether enrollment gains are genuine engagement or inertia.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a concrete, causally identified effect size for the default effect on enrollment and, crucially, explains the mechanism (present bias, cognitive cost) — informing whether the lift from an opt-out design in our app is likely to be real behavior change or mere inertia, and whether it persists in attitudes afterward.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment assigning employees to different default/enrollment varieties of a salary-linked savings account, with follow-up interventions isolating mechanisms.

**Limitations.**

- salary-linked account for employees, not a self-directed mobile banking app
- developing-country context, different regulatory and liquidity environment
- no long-run (6-12 month) withdrawal or amount-saved data reported
