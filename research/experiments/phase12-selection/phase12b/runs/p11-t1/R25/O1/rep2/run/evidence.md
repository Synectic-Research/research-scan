## 1. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a 4,207-person sample from five European countries, default nudges significantly shift risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes, but implementing a default (especially one the target refuses) significantly lowers how trustworthy/honest the advice is rated, an effect mediated by perceived wealth-manager honesty.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer financial-advice setting testing exactly when a default feels helpful versus manipulative, with quantified reputational costs — the most directly usable evidence for the entry-design decision.

**Why it matters here.** Gives EU-representative evidence that a default which is refused or one-size-fits-all reads as less honest — the exact dark-pattern/reputational risk the brief flags — and shows question framing (e.g., 'how much to invest' vs 'how much to leave safe') independently shifts choices, directly informing both the entry-design and slider-wording decisions.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative multi-country European survey experiment on wealth-management default framing and question wording.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management risk-allocation decisions, not round-up savings specifically
- does not measure long-run behavioral persistence, only immediate choice and perception

## 2. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouded the minimum-payment autopay option cut minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because nudged users picked autopay amounts barely above the minimum and some lost autopay protection, increasing missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). A rare consumer-financial field experiment showing a default-like nudge changes nominal behavior without changing the real outcome, with heterogeneity by liquidity — the closest analogue to the brief's core worry.

**Why it matters here.** Directly supports the support team's concern: a choice-architecture change can move a headline enrollment/behavior metric without moving the thing that actually matters (debt/savings amount), and can create new harm (missed payments) for liquidity-constrained users — exactly the trade-off the round-up entry decision must weigh.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit card accounts with 6-month follow-up on payment behavior and debt outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not a savings product
- does not test opt-out vs opt-in vs active-choice framing directly, only a shrouding nudge

## 3. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and theoretical framework for downstream (post-adoption) default effects, showing they depend on time course of choice/consumption, consumers' antecedent preferences, and salience of trade-offs — resolving inconsistent prior findings.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). The literature's own general-purpose framework for exactly the persistence/disengagement question the brief asks about, in a consumer (not employer) frame.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the team a structured way to predict and measure which users will disengage after opt-out enrollment (weak antecedent preference, low salience) versus which will actually use the pot — directly shapes what to instrument and watch for in the round-up rollout.

**Method.** Conceptual synthesis and framework-building across the consumer default-effects literature; abstract-only for empirical specifics.

**Limitations.**

- general consumer choice-architecture theory, not financial-product specific
- no effect sizes since it's a conceptual synthesis, not primary data

## 4. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · computational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** In 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions, 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out cost together (friction complementarity); adding a 15% tip option to the 20/25/30% menu captures 69% of achievable consumer welfare gain at only 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Explicit method transfer: a structural friction/menu-design model for preset-option adherence and welfare trade-offs, directly usable for the multiplier-level decision even though the underlying transactions are taxi tips.

**Why it matters here.** Provides a transferable method — a structural decomposition of why people accept a preset menu option and a counterfactual tool for choosing which value to add/preset — directly applicable to deciding which round-up multiplier (1x/2x/5x) to preset and what a welfare-minimizing menu looks like.

**Method.** Structural model estimated on 8.6 million transaction records plus an independent stated-preference survey to identify true preferences separate from default influence.

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions, not a financial savings product
- no persistence or longitudinal outcome measured, single-decision setting

## 5. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts cuts unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, implying market-wide savings of £170-240 million.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Same consumer-banking, self-directed, field-experiment setting as the product being built, showing default enrollment into a bank feature at scale changes real financial outcomes.

**Why it matters here.** Demonstrates that automatic (opt-out style) enrollment into a low-friction banking feature produces large, quantifiable consumer financial benefits in the exact UK consumer-banking setting the round-up feature will operate in.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments at major UK banks.

**Limitations.**

- studies alert enrollment, not a savings contribution decision
- does not report the alert feature's own disengagement or opt-out rate

## 6. Regulating Dark Patterns

Martin Brenncke · 2023 · Aston Publications Explorer (Aston University) · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2310.00340>

**Key finding.** Maps EU laws on dark patterns to a taxonomy of six autonomy-violation categories, showing EU regulation of pre-selected defaults and similar practices is grounded in protecting consumer autonomy.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C5 distributional and manipulation risk (3/3). Gives the EU regulatory framework for judging whether a default reads as helpful or manipulative, the reputational risk the brief flags explicitly.

**Why it matters here.** Directly informs whether an opt-out enrollment design risks being classified as a dark pattern under EU law, which should shape the choice between opt-out and active-choice designs.

**Method.** Legal/doctrinal analysis and normative taxonomy construction; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- legal/normative analysis, not empirical
- no effect sizes on enrollment or engagement
- not specific to financial products

## 7. Automatic Enrollment with a 12% Default Contribution Rate

John Beshears, Ruofei Guo, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31601>

**Key finding.** With a 12% default contribution rate, 25% of employees had not opted out after 12 months (versus ~50% non-opt-out under lower defaults elsewhere), and non-opters anchored at an income-suboptimal 12% rate.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly tests anchoring on a high default level and 12-month opt-out persistence, close in structure to the round-up multiplier decision even though the setting is a retirement plan.

**Why it matters here.** Shows the default level itself shapes both enrollment persistence and anchoring, evidence for how a high pre-set round-up multiplier could both raise and distort savings behavior.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from a firm with an unusually high default contribution rate; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored 401(k) plan, not a consumer app
- single-firm case study

## 8. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment pushed 401(k) participation above 85% at three firms, but about 80% of participants initially accepted both the low default savings rate (2-3%) and conservative default fund, and half still did so after three years, with offsetting effects on wealth accumulation.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The classic anchoring-on-default-level study the current literature still argues with; directly informs the level/preset decision even though the setting is dated and employer-based.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration that a pre-set default level anchors most people at that exact level for years — the direct precedent for worrying that a pre-selected round-up multiplier will anchor most users rather than let them choose their true preferred level.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative panel data from three large firms' 401(k) plans.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) plan with employer-selected defaults, not a consumer app
- predates the fintech/EU regulatory context the brief says has since changed

## 9. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A randomized nudge (credit card payment reminders) on a Brazilian financial management platform cut late-payment fees by 14% but raised overdraft fees by 9%, with users who had a history of overdraft use experiencing a net 5% fee increase while other users saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). A closely analogous fintech field experiment showing heterogeneous, sometimes harmful, effects of a helpful-seeming nudge — exactly the distributional risk the brief asks the team to check for before defaulting everyone in.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a well-intentioned nudge in a consumer fintech app can help most users while making a vulnerable subgroup worse off, directly the distributional risk the brief warns against building for on average.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment with a consumer financial management platform in Brazil.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrollment defaults or round-up savings specifically
- single-country consumer credit context, not an EU savings app

## 10. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrollment raised participation above 85% at three firms, but about 80% of participants accepted the low default contribution rate (2-3%) and default fund, with half still at the default after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The foundational default-and-anchoring study that all subsequent enrollment-design literature, including this project's question, argues with or extends.

**Why it matters here.** The classic demonstration that defaults both massively raise nominal enrollment and strongly anchor the contribution level for years, directly the tension the brief poses between enrollment lift and just relocating people to a low pre-set level.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative panel data from three large firms comparing automatic versus standard enrollment.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored 401(k), not a consumer app
- pre-dates the mobile/EU regulatory context the brief says has changed the calculus
