## 1. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and conceptual framework for 'downstream' default effects, synthesizing when defaults' immediate effects persist, fade, or reverse across subsequent consumer decisions.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). General consumer-behavior framework for downstream default persistence, matching the brief's core question of whether the enrollment lift lasts at 6-12 months.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a general model for predicting whether our round-up default's initial enrollment lift will persist or decay, and what factors moderate that — directly informs how we should measure success beyond signup, not just enrollment rate.

**Method.** Conceptual review and theoretical framework paper synthesizing the consumer default-effect literature. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical/conceptual, not a new empirical estimate
- not specific to financial products
- abstract gives no effect sizes

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a representative five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges produced medium-to-large effects on investment risk-taking but also lowered ratings of the wealth manager's advice — especially when the default was refused — with perceived honesty mediating the effect.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU-based experimental evidence on default effect size, level framing/anchoring, and perceived manipulation — matches nearly every open question in the brief for a consumer financial-advice context.

**Why it matters here.** Direct EU evidence that defaults can be perceived as less trustworthy or manipulative when refused, and that presentation framing shifts the level people choose — bears squarely on both the reputational-risk and anchoring questions the brief raises for the round-up entry and level decisions.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative multi-country European survey experiment (n=4207) on default-framed investment allocation.

**Limitations.**

- investment risk allocation setting, not round-up savings specifically
- one-shot experimental ratings, no field or behavioral persistence data

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrollment and default auto-escalation: steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of employees escalate on their first scheduled date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving long-term, exactly the persistence evidence the brief asked for.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of our support team's objection: the enrollment lift is real at signup but its effect on actual saving shrinks dramatically over time — directly bearing on whether opt-out enrollment is a vanity metric.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans accounting for job separation, withdrawal at separation, and auto-escalation opt-out.

**Limitations.**

- retirement 401(k) plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated setting

## 4. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the autopay-minimum option reduced minimum-only payers by 23% but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, with nudged cardholders enrolling autopay at only slightly above the minimum.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Consumer financial product with liquid dynamics (credit card) showing default/nudge effects on nominal enrollment diverge from effects on the real financial outcome — the exact caution the brief raises about the round-up feature.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the support team's fear: a nudge that shifts nominal enrollment/payment behavior does not change the underlying financial outcome — critical to whether opt-out enrollment changes real saving or just who is nominally enrolled.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit cards with 6-month follow-up using administrative payment data.

**Limitations.**

- Credit card debt paydown, not savings enrollment
- no data beyond 6 months
- not a savings-app setting

## 5. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale field experiments automatically enrolling U.K. bank customers into just-in-time overdraft text alerts reduced unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in market-wide annual savings, though alerts captured less than half of potential avoidable savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Same industry and delivery channel as the product being built, showing a consumer-bank automatic-enrollment default working at scale without employer trust as the mechanism.

**Why it matters here.** Demonstrates that automatic (opt-out-style) enrollment into a bank-app feature works at scale without an employer intermediary, directly supporting the feasibility case for opt-out entry into the round-up feature in the same consumer-banking channel.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments run directly through major U.K. retail banks' own apps/systems.

**Limitations.**

- measures a different feature (alerts, not savings enrollment)
- no data on retention or amounts saved over 6-12 months

## 6. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model decomposing default adherence into deviation cost and opt-out cost, estimated on 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions, finds 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of both frictions, and that adding an intermediate preset option (15%) captures 69% of achievable welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (2/3). Explicit structural method for how preset menu options anchor choices and how welfare gains from menu redesign are quantified — a direct method transfer to the round-up multiplier preset-level decision despite the differing product.

**Why it matters here.** Its friction-decomposition method and menu-redesign counterfactual transfer directly to our preset multiplier decision: it shows how adding an intermediate preset level between existing options can capture most of the achievable welfare gain without changing underlying frictions — directly actionable for deciding among 1x/2x/5x and which to pre-select.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on 8.6 million real tipping transactions plus a stated-preference survey, with counterfactual menu-design simulations.

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions, not financial savings
- single deviation-cost/opt-out-cost model may not capture savings-specific psychology
- no persistence or withdrawal outcomes measured

## 8. The Behavioral Foundations of Default Effects: Theory and Evidence from Medicare Part D

Zarek Brot-Goldberg, Timothy Layton, Boris Vabson, Adelina Yanyue Wang · 2023 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210013>

**Key finding.** Default rules in Medicare Part D have large, persistent effects on enrollment and drug utilization, and beneficiary passivity is highly insensitive even to substantial losses from staying with a bad default, with active-choice variation driven by transitory shocks rather than attentiveness.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Strong, persistent default-effect evidence with a distributional focus on low-income beneficiaries, relevant to the brief's concern about who is helped or hurt by defaults, despite the different domain.

**Why it matters here.** Shows default persistence can be extremely strong and largely insensitive to outcome value — a caution against assuming our round-up default's effect will fade the way the retirement literature suggests, and a warning that passivity can mask real harm for those defaulted badly.

**Method.** Three natural experiments using random assignment to default options among low-income Medicare beneficiaries.

**Limitations.**

- health insurance/drug benefit setting, not consumer savings
- government administrative program, not a self-directed app
- no financial contribution-amount analogy

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** In a Brazilian fintech field experiment, payment reminders cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised checking-account overdraft fees by 9%, concentrated among users with a history of overdraft use, so overdraft-prone users saw a net 5% increase in fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). A consumer fintech RCT demonstrating heterogeneous, sometimes harmful, effects of a nudge, directly supporting the brief's concern about who is helped versus hurt by design defaults.

**Why it matters here.** Shows directly, in a comparable consumer fintech setting, that a well-intentioned nudge can help most users while actively harming a vulnerable subgroup — the exact distributional failure mode the brief warns against building unknowingly.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment on a consumer financial-management platform in Brazil.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrollment defaults or round-up savings specifically
- single-country, single-platform sample
