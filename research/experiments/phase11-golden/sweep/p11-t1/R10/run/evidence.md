## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge shrouding the minimum-payment autopay default cut minimum-only paying by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, since nudged users set autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum or dropped autopay altogether.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Consumer financial nudge experiment showing default-driven behavior change decoupled from substantive outcomes, directly analogous to the enrollment-vs-amount-saved question.

**Why it matters here.** Consumer, self-directed, liquid-product evidence that a default/nudge can move a headline enrollment-like metric without moving the outcome that matters, the exact 'vanity enrollment' risk raised for the round-up feature.

**Method.** Field experiment with credit card customers, six-month follow-up on payment and enrollment behavior.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not savings enrollment
- does not directly test opt-in vs opt-out vs active choice entry design

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Default nudges on investment risk-taking produce medium-to-large behavioral effects but also lower users' rating of the advice and advisor honesty, especially when the individual ultimately rejects the default; question framing (asking about risky vs. safe amounts) produces smaller shifts.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU-based experimental evidence on when a financial default is perceived as manipulative, directly informing the dark-pattern and level-framing decisions.

**Why it matters here.** EU experimental evidence on when a financial default reads as manipulative rather than helpful, directly informing the dark-pattern reputational risk and the framing of the round-up multiplier choice.

**Method.** Three experiments including a large representative sample across five European countries (n=4207).

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management advice setting, not a banking app round-up feature specifically
- single-session experimental ratings, not real enrollment or behavior over time

## 3. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrollment pushed participation above 85%, but roughly 80% of participants initially accepted both the low default contribution rate and conservative default fund, and half remained at the default rate after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The classic study demonstrating that preset default levels anchor final contribution choices, the foundational work the anchoring literature builds on.

**Why it matters here.** The classic demonstration that a preset default level strongly anchors the ultimately chosen contribution rate, the foundational evidence base for the anchoring question even though set in an employer plan.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms' 401(k) plans.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) plans, not a consumer app
- predates current mobile/fintech and EU regulatory context
- no publication year given, an older foundational study

## 4. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Steady-state saving rate gains from auto-enrollment (0.6% of income) and auto-escalation (0.3%) are much smaller than commonly assumed once job turnover, pre-vesting withdrawal, and opt-out from escalation are modeled; only 40% of auto-escalation defaults actually escalate on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Quantifies how a default-driven enrollment lift erodes over time, the core of the brief's persistence question, in a well-cited recent NBER paper.

**Why it matters here.** Directly supports the support team's skepticism that the enrollment lift is a vanity metric that shrinks once realistic attrition, opt-out, and withdrawal are accounted for.

**Method.** Dynamic structural analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating employee turnover and withdrawal behavior; abstract-only for full method detail.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) plans, not a liquid consumer round-up product
- U.S. retirement system, different regulatory and liquidity context

## 5. Default Options and Retirement Saving Dynamics

Taha Choukhmane · 2025 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210881>

**Key finding.** Auto-enrollment's participation and contribution gains, replicated at 12 months, attenuate substantially by 36 months, though it durably reduces savings inequality; a life-cycle model fits the pattern with a switching cost of about $250.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Quantifies attenuation of the default enrollment effect over time, central to whether the enrollment lift persists.

**Why it matters here.** The most direct recent evidence for the persistence question: shows how much a default's apparent lift decays over a medium horizon, and that inequality reduction rather than aggregate savings is the durable effect.

**Method.** Panel analysis of 100+ US retirement plans plus a representative UK panel; structural life-cycle consumption-savings model.

**Limitations.**

- employer retirement plans, not a liquid consumer product
- longest horizon reported is 36 months, not directly 6-12 months

## 9. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Field experiments show 'active choice' (no default, forced decision) increases behavior-change rates, and an 'enhanced active choice' variant that highlights losses from the non-preferred option performs even better, recommended as a complement to automatic enrollment or a substitute when defaults are infeasible or unethical.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). Foundational paper introducing and testing 'active choice' and 'enhanced active choice' as alternatives to default enrollment, the specific method being weighed in the entry-design decision.

**Why it matters here.** The origin paper for the 'active choice' entry design the brief is weighing against opt-in/opt-out; its enhanced active choice technique (framing losses in the non-chosen option) is a concrete, transferable method for the onboarding screen.

**Method.** Series of studies including two field experiments testing active choice and enhanced active choice interventions across savings and health-behavior contexts.

**Limitations.**

- some experiments are in medication-adherence rather than financial-savings contexts
- older paper (2011), predates app-based fintech and current EU dark-pattern scrutiny
- abstract gives no 6-month persistence or engagement data

## 10. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Only about 15% of individuals are 'active savers' who respond to subsidies mainly by shifting assets ($0.01 of new saving per $1 of subsidy); the other 85% are passive savers unmoved by incentives but strongly responsive to automatic contributions made on their behalf.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Foundational large-scale distinction between active and passive savers underpinning who benefits from automatic contributions vs. active choice, directly informing the distributional-risk question.

**Why it matters here.** Foundational large-scale evidence for the active-vs-passive distinction underlying the opt-in/opt-out/active-choice decision, showing who defaults actually help versus who they don't.

**Method.** Population-level administrative data for Denmark (41 million observations) exploiting policy variation in subsidies versus automatic contributions.

**Limitations.**

- tax-advantaged retirement accounts, not a liquid consumer savings feature
- Danish administrative/tax context differs from EU app regulatory environment
- predates current fintech/app-based savings tools
