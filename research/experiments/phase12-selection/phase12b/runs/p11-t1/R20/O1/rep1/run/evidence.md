## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** Shrouding the autopay-minimum-payment option cut the share of cardholders paying exactly the minimum by 23% after six months but did not reduce credit-card debt, and reduced autopay enrollment increased missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Consumer, self-directed financial-app setting testing a default/nudge that changes enrollment but not the outcome that matters, matching the brief's central worry.

**Why it matters here.** Directly undercuts the 'default enrollment = better outcomes' assumption: a nudge that shifted enrolment behavior did not translate into the intended financial benefit, and reduced auto-enrollment increased costly errors — exactly the support team's worry about our round-up defaults.

**Method.** Field experiment (nudge) on live credit-card accounts with six-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- Credit-card debt-repayment context, not a savings product
- No measure of outcomes beyond 6 months

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Default nudges shift risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes across five European consumer samples (n=4207), but also lower how users rate the advisor's advice, especially when the default is rejected, and how the default is framed further shifts behavior.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer financial-decision experiment giving effect sizes for both the default's behavioral pull and its perceived-manipulation cost — exactly the trade-off the brief flags as a reputational risk.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a default can move behavior strongly while simultaneously damaging perceived trustworthiness — directly relevant to the reputational-risk concern about the round-up default reading as manipulative, and to how the multiplier field should be framed and worded.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative-sample experiment across five European countries, on investment/savings default framing.

**Limitations.**

- Wealth-management investment-risk context rather than round-up savings specifically
- Advice-rating measure is a proxy for trust, not actual disengagement or complaints

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the effect of automatic enrollment and auto-escalation: steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of auto-escalation defaults are kept at the first escalation date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving, with quantified effect sizes, which the brief explicitly asked us to look for.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of our support team's objection: default enrolment's effect is much smaller once turnover, withdrawal, and later opt-out are accounted for — directly bears on whether an opt-out round-up default produces durable saving or just nominal, short-lived enrolment.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job turnover, balance withdrawal at separation, and opt-out from auto-escalation.

**Limitations.**

- Retirement plans (401k) rather than liquid consumer savings
- Employer-mediated auto-escalation mechanism does not map onto a self-directed app setting

## 4. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework showing downstream default effects depend on the time course of choice/consumption, prior preferences, and salience of trade-offs, and are not uniform or guaranteed to persist.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). A 2025 synthesis of exactly the persistence-of-default-effects question the brief foregrounds, in a general consumer setting.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a framework for predicting when a default's initial enrollment effect will or won't persist into real engagement, and flags what to measure (time course, preference antecedents) to test it in-app before committing to opt-out.

**Method.** Conceptual synthesis and research-agenda paper drawing on the choice-architecture literature. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- Conceptual/theoretical, not a specific empirical estimate
- Not specific to financial products

## 5. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** In 8.6 million taxi tipping decisions, 84% of default adherence comes from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out cost, and removing either friction alone would not sustain adherence; adding an intermediate tip option captured 69% of achievable welfare gain at low revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (2/3). Explicit method transfer: the friction-decomposition model of why defaults stick is directly usable to reason about our opt-out design and multiplier menu, even though the tested product is a taxi tip.

**Why it matters here.** Names the specific mechanism — deviation cost plus opt-out cost — that makes any preset default 'sticky', which transfers directly to designing how easy it should be to change the round-up multiplier or exit the feature, and suggests an intermediate preset captures most of the achievable benefit cheaply.

**Method.** Structural model estimated on 8.6M transactions, validated against a stated-preference survey of true preferences.

**Limitations.**

- Consumer tipping decisions, not a savings product
- Single-transaction choice rather than an ongoing enrolment decision

## 7. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment raised 401(k) participation above 85% at three firms, but about 80% of enrollees initially accepted both the low default contribution rate and conservative default fund, and half still did at three years, roughly offsetting the wealth gains from higher participation on average, while substantially reducing non-participation in the lower tail.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The classic study everything on default anchoring and enrollment in this list responds to; establishes the anchoring mechanism the brief worries about for the multiplier choice.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration that a preset default level anchors most people for years, which is the central risk in shipping a pre-set round-up multiplier rather than a blank field — later contradicting studies argue directly with this one.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms' 401(k) plans, multi-year follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- Employer 401(k) plans, not a consumer app
- Predates the fintech/liquid-savings and current EU regulatory context

## 8. Automatic Enrollment with a 12% Default Contribution Rate

John Beshears, Ruofei Guo, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31601>

**Key finding.** With a 12% default contribution rate, 25% of employees had opted out after 12 months (versus roughly half in plans with lower defaults), and many anchored at the unmatched, likely-suboptimal 12% rate, especially lower earners.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Quantifies default-driven anchoring at a high preset contribution level, relevant to the multiplier decision.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a high preset default both suppresses opt-out and anchors many participants at an economically suboptimal level, a direct caution for choosing which round-up multiplier to preselect.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from an employer retirement plan compared against a literature review of lower-default plans.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored retirement plan, not a self-directed liquid consumer product
- no employer-matching mechanism analogous to our product exists

## 10. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrolment raised participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants initially accepted low default savings rates (2-3%) and conservative default funds, with half still at the default rate after three years, so higher participation did not translate proportionally into higher savings.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Foundational evidence that default enrolment gains can be undermined by anchoring at the preset level, central to the multiplier decision.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration that default-driven enrolment gains can be offset by anchoring at a low default rate, directly cautioning against a low preselected round-up multiplier and showing enrolment counts can mask stagnant amounts saved.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms tracking automatic-enrollment cohorts over several years.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) plan, not a liquid consumer product
- predates fintech app context and EU regulation
