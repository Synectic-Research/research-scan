## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouds the minimum-payment autopay default cut exact-minimum-payment behavior by 23% over six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because liquidity-constrained cardholders simply chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Consumer financial field experiment showing a default/nudge changes surface behavior without changing the underlying financial outcome, the core 'vanity metric' concern in the brief.

**Why it matters here.** Directly supports the support team's worry: a choice-architecture change can shift nominal enrollment/behavior sharply while leaving the real outcome (debt/savings) unchanged, which is exactly the enrollment-vs-amount distinction the brief asks us to test before defaulting everyone into round-ups.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit cards, six-month follow-up, National Bureau of Economic Research working paper.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not savings accumulation
- shrouding a payment option differs structurally from opt-in/opt-out/active-choice enrollment

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a representative five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges produced medium-to-large effects on risk-taking, but implementing a default -- especially one the client then refuses -- significantly lowered how trustworthy and honest the advice was perceived to be.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly tests when a financial default is perceived as manipulative in a European consumer setting, the manipulation-risk question the brief asks about.

**Why it matters here.** Empirically shows the exact reputational trade-off the brief flags: a default that moves behavior can simultaneously read as manipulative and damage perceived trust, which should directly weigh against opt-out and toward active-choice or careful framing for entry design.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, including a large representative multi-country European experiment, on financial default framing in wealth management.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management advice context, not a mobile round-up feature
- risk-taking allocation decisions, not enrollment/withdrawal from a savings pot

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Accounting for job turnover, vesting, and opt-outs, automatic enrollment raises steady-state savings by only 0.6% of income and auto-escalation by 0.3%, with only 40% of employees escalating on their first scheduled date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the 'defaults always help' premise with quantified medium/long-run attenuation, exactly the evidence the brief asked us to find.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantitative statement of the support team's objection: once you follow people past enrollment, automatic-default effects on real savings amounts are much smaller than the headline enrollment-rate lift suggests.

**Method.** Analysis of administrative data from nine 401(k) plans incorporating medium- and long-run dynamics (turnover, withdrawal, opt-out).

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated auto-escalation differs from a self-set multiplier

## 4. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and conceptual framework for when default effects persist, fade, or reverse downstream, driven by the time course of choice/consumption, consumers' antecedent preferences, and salience of trade-offs.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). General-purpose consumer psychology framework for exactly the downstream persistence/disengagement question the brief raises.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a concrete list of moderators (antecedent preferences, trade-off salience, time course) the team should measure when evaluating whether round-up enrollment defaults persist past sign-up -- directly shapes what the 6-12 month follow-up study should track.

**Method.** Theoretical synthesis and taxonomy-building across the consumer-default literature. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- conceptual framework, not a new empirical estimate
- not specific to financial products

## 5. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** In 8.6 million tipping decisions, 84% of default adherence arose from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out effort cost together (friction complementarity); adding a lower option (15%) below an existing 20-25-30% menu captured 69% of achievable consumer welfare gains at only 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Named method transfer: a structural default-menu model decomposing deviation cost and opt-out cost, plus a counterfactual on adding a lower preset tier, applies directly to designing the round-up multiplier menu.

**Why it matters here.** The counterfactual on adding a lower preset option is a direct, quantified answer to the multiplier-menu question: it shows how much welfare is left on the table by a restrictive menu and how cheaply it can be recovered, a method that transfers straightforwardly to designing the 1x/2x/5x round-up menu.

**Method.** Structural model estimated on 8.6 million real transactions plus an independent stated-preference survey of preferences.

**Limitations.**

- taxi tipping, not a savings product
- monetary stakes and decision frequency differ substantially from a savings sweep

## 7. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrollment pushed participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants initially accepted both the low default savings rate (2-3%) and conservative default fund, and half still held both defaults after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The classic result on anchoring at a pre-set contribution level, the direct precedent for the multiplier pre-selection decision.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration that a pre-set contribution level anchors most participants for years, directly warning that whichever multiplier (1x/2x/5x) is pre-selected in the round-up feature is likely to become the de facto ceiling for most users.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative panel data from three large firms.

**Limitations.**

- no publication year given, indicating this is the classic study current literature argues with

## 8. The Behavioral Foundations of Default Effects: Theory and Evidence from Medicare Part D

Zarek Brot-Goldberg, Timothy Layton, Boris Vabson, Adelina Yanyue Wang · 2023 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210013>

**Key finding.** Default rules in Medicare Part D have large, persistent effects on enrollment and drug utilization, and beneficiary passivity is extremely insensitive to the value of the default option, even when it causes substantial losses.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Rigorous natural-experiment evidence on default persistence and insensitivity to value, informing how much weight to put on opt-out enrollment numbers.

**Why it matters here.** Strong causal evidence that default persistence can reflect true inattention rather than considered choice -- a caution against assuming opt-out enrollment reflects genuine preference, relevant to the manipulation-risk question.

**Method.** Three natural experiments using random assignment to default options in a large administrative claims setting.

**Limitations.**

- government health-insurance enrollment, not a consumer fintech product
- no employer-style trusted intermediary but also no app/liquidity context

## 9. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Two field experiments show 'active choice' (forcing a yes/no decision) increases desired behavior versus no intervention, and a refined 'enhanced active choice' that frames losses in the non-preferred option performs even better, recommended as a complement to or substitute for automatic enrollment.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). The core paper testing whether active choice (and an enhanced variant) gets most of the default's benefit, which is exactly the brief's stated open question.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a concrete, tested alternative to full opt-out enrolment — forced active choice with loss-framed messaging — that could capture much of the enrolment lift without passively sweeping in disengaged users, directly informing the entry-mechanism decision.

**Method.** Series of studies including two field experiments comparing active choice, enhanced active choice, and no-default conditions.

**Limitations.**

- field experiments are in health/medication and general savings contexts, not a mobile banking round-up feature
- no 6-12 month persistence data reported in the abstract
- enhanced active choice framing itself risks reading as manipulative under EU dark-pattern scrutiny

## 10. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrolment raised participation to over 85%, but roughly 80% of participants initially accepted the low default contribution rate (2-3%) and default fund, and half still did so after three years, roughly offsetting the participation gain against a lower savings rate per person.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Foundational evidence on anchoring at a preset default level, the exact mechanism the brief worries about for the round-up multiplier decision.

**Why it matters here.** The classic demonstration that a low preset default anchors contribution levels for years, directly warning against shipping a low pre-selected round-up multiplier if the goal is meaningful savings amounts rather than nominal enrolment.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms tracking automatic-enrolment participants for up to three years.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) plan, not a self-directed consumer app
- over two decades old, predating the app/liquidity/regulatory context the brief flags as changed
- does not measure withdrawal or disengagement, only contribution-rate anchoring
