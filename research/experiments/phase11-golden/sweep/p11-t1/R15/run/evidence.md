## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** Shrouding the credit-card autopay-minimum option reduced minimum-only payers by 23% after six months, but did not significantly reduce debt because nudged cardholders picked autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Consumer financial field experiment showing a default shifts behavior nominally without changing the real financial outcome, directly bearing on the amount-vs-enrolment and anchoring questions.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a default-style nudge can shift nominal behavior while anchoring people just above a floor and failing to move the outcome that matters (debt/savings amount) — a direct caution for a preset multiplier and for treating enrolment numbers as success.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit-card autopay enrollment with six-month follow-up on payment behavior and debt outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- credit-card debt paydown context, not a savings product
- does not directly test opt-in/opt-out/active-choice enrolment designs

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a representative five-European-country experiment (n=4207), default nudges shifted risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes, but also lowered ratings of the advisor's advice, especially when the default was refused, and how the default was framed shifted the amount invested.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU experimental evidence on default framing's effect on chosen level and on perceived manipulativeness, directly actionable for both open design decisions.

**Why it matters here.** Directly informs both open decisions: shows defaults strongly move the level people end up at (relevant to preset-multiplier anchoring) and gives EU-based evidence that imposing or refusing a default lowers perceived trustworthiness — a direct signal of dark-pattern risk.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative multi-country online experiment testing default presence, framing, and risk-taking outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- investment/wealth-management risk-taking context rather than a round-up savings feature
- no 6-12 month persistence data

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from automatic enrollment and 0.3% from default auto-escalation, and only 40% of people with an auto-escalation default actually escalate on the first date, with more opting out later.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the 'defaults always help' premise with quantified medium/long-run erosion of the default effect.

**Why it matters here.** Directly supports the support team's objection that defaults' apparent enrolment lift is much smaller in steady state than short-run figures suggest, once real-world attrition and opt-out are accounted for.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job turnover, unvested matches, withdrawal upon separation, and auto-escalation opt-out.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) employer retirement plans, not a liquid consumer savings app
- no evidence on round-up-style micro-savings products specifically

## 4. Opt-in or Opt-out? The Power of Defaults in Pension Enrollment Choices

Tabea Bucher-Koenen, Luisa Wallossek, Joachim Winter · 2026 · CESifo working papers · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.65864/lhwgnu4zpv>

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment for low-income employees in Germany's public pension system raised participation by 23 percentage points, though most people who preferred otherwise actively opted out, and the default effect was strongest under high incentives, complex choices, and low awareness of enrollment status.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly compares opt-in/opt-out default effects with a quantified size and heterogeneity, closely analogous to the entry-design decision.

**Why it matters here.** Quantifies the opt-in vs opt-out default effect size and shows it is moderated by incentive strength, choice complexity, and awareness — directly relevant to predicting whether our round-up default will be seen as helpful or simply reversed.

**Method.** Natural experiment using nationwide administrative and survey data around Germany's auto-enrollment rollout.

**Limitations.**

- public pension enrollment, not a consumer fintech product
- no experimental active-choice condition, inferred only from opt-out rates

## 5. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrollment pushed participation above 85% at three firms, but about 80% of participants initially accepted the low default contribution rate (2-3%) and conservative default fund, with half still anchored there after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Foundational anchoring evidence that current literature (e.g., Choukhmane 2025, Choi et al. 2024) directly argues with.

**Why it matters here.** The classic demonstration that default enrolment anchors people at the preset level rather than freeing optimal choice — the foundational evidence behind the concern that a preset multiplier will anchor most users regardless of what they'd otherwise pick.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms with automatic enrollment, tracked over multiple years.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) plans, predating today's fintech/app context and EU regulatory climate
- an older foundational study the brief flags as superseded by newer work

## 7. Default Options and Retirement Saving Dynamics

Taha Choukhmane · 2025 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210881>

**Key finding.** Auto-enrollment's boost to participation and contributions at 12 months is substantially attenuated by 36 months, with modest average savings gains at that horizon (though inequality in savings falls), and a life-cycle model estimates a switching cost of about $250.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best available recent quantification of default-effect attenuation over time, directly informing the persistence question even from an adjacent setting.

**Why it matters here.** Provides a recent, rigorous quantification of exactly the 'does the default effect persist at 6-12+ months' question the brief asks, even though set in employer retirement plans.

**Method.** Analysis of over 100 US retirement plans plus a UK panel, combined with a structural life-cycle consumption-savings model.

**Limitations.**

- employer retirement plans, not a liquid consumer savings app
- 36-month horizon still longer than typical liquid-savings usage patterns in an app

## 8. Employer-Based Short-Term Savings Accounts

Sarah Holmes Berk, John Beshears, Jay Garg, James J. Choi et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32074>

**Key finding.** An opt-in payroll-deduction short-term (liquid, anytime-withdrawable) savings account at five UK firms saw take-up of no more than 0.7% of eligible employees, though among those who activated, 96% still had a balance and 87% received an automatic contribution at month 12.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Closest analog on opt-in take-up and persistence for a liquid, withdrawable savings product, directly informative for the entry-design decision.

**Why it matters here.** The closest available analog to our liquid round-up product: shows opt-in alone yields near-zero enrolment despite strong retention among the few who opt in, arguing against relying on pure opt-in if broad enrolment matters, while showing persistence isn't inherently a problem once someone actively chooses.

**Method.** Field study of a payroll-deduction short-term savings program at five UK firms, tracked over the first 12 months post-activation.

**Limitations.**

- employer payroll-deduction channel, not direct-to-consumer app enrolment
- no opt-out or active-choice comparison condition tested

## 9. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A randomized reminder nudge in a Brazilian financial-management platform cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised checking-account overdraft fees by 9%, with users who had a history of overdraft use experiencing a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Consumer, self-directed fintech field experiment showing that a well-intentioned nudge can help the average user while harming an identifiable subgroup — direct evidence for the brief's 'who is helped and who is hurt' concern (C5), in the right kind of setting (C4).

**Why it matters here.** Shows concretely that a consumer-facing financial nudge can help most users while making a vulnerable subgroup worse off — exactly the distributional risk the brief flags for round-up defaults, and a template for the kind of heterogeneity analysis the team should run before declaring the feature a success.

**Method.** Field experiment (randomized intervention) with a consumer financial-management platform, tracking downstream fee outcomes across linked accounts.

**Limitations.**

- studies a payment reminder, not an enrollment default or contribution-level anchor
- no measure of enrollment or opt-out behavior, so C1/C6 don't transfer directly
- single-market (Brazil) platform; EU regulatory context not addressed

## 10. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Introduces and field-tests 'enhanced active choice' (highlighting losses in the non-preferred option) as an alternative to automatic enrollment, recommending it as a complement or substitute when opt-out defaults are infeasible or unethical.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). Directly originates and tests the active-choice design that is one of the two enrollment options the brief is deciding between, making it foundational to the C6 comparison even though its empirical setting is health care rather than consumer finance.

**Why it matters here.** This is the originating paper on the exact technique — active/enhanced active choice as an enrollment design — that the brief's Decision 1 is choosing between; it directly informs whether active choice can substitute for opt-out without the same ethical/manipulation exposure.

**Method.** Narrative review plus a series of studies including two field experiments testing active choice and enhanced active choice against status-quo/default framings; abstract-only, primarily in a medication-adherence context.

**Limitations.**

- field experiments are in health/medication-adherence settings, not consumer financial products
- no reported persistence or amount-saved outcomes at 6-12 months
- abstract gives no effect sizes for the active-choice comparison
- outside the recency window; predates app-based fintech and EU dark-pattern scrutiny
