## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the autopay-minimum default reduced flat minimum-payment enrollment by 23% at 6 months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, and increased missed payments among liquidity-constrained cardholders.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest real consumer-financial field experiment testing whether a default/nudge in a self-directed banking product changes both enrollment and actual financial amounts, revealing harm to a subgroup.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests whether nudging users into a different default payment behavior actually changes financial outcomes in a self-directed consumer financial product, showing the enrollment/behavior gap and harm to a subgroup that our Entry decision must weigh.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit cards with 6-month follow-up; behavioral analysis of autopay enrollment and payment amounts.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt repayment rather than savings enrollment
- outcome is autopay minimum payment behavior, not a round-up feature

## 2. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and conceptual framework for 'downstream' default effects on consumer behavior, identifying time-course of choice/consumption, antecedent preferences, and trade-off salience as key moderators of whether default effects persist or fade.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). The most recent synthesis specifically about downstream (persistence, disengagement) effects of defaults in consumer behavior, giving a measurement framework rather than a single effect size.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a structured way to predict and measure whether the round-up default's enrollment effect will persist or decay, flagging exactly the moderators our post-launch measurement plan should track.

**Method.** Conceptual review and framework synthesis of the consumer-default literature; abstract-only detail on individual studies.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical/synthesis paper, not a new empirical estimate
- not specific to financial products

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the effect of automatic enrollment and auto-escalation defaults on 401(k) savings: steady-state saving rates rise by only 0.6% of income from automatic enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of auto-escalation defaults are followed at the first escalation date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The strongest available quantified contradiction of the 'defaults always help' premise, precisely the counter-evidence the brief asks for.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified rebuttal of the premise that defaults reliably and durably lift savings, directly supporting the support team's 'vanity metric' concern once turnover, withdrawal, and later opt-out are accounted for.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating employee turnover, withdrawal upon separation, and auto-escalation opt-out over multiple years.

**Limitations.**

- retirement 401(k) plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- abstract gives aggregate effect sizes but not distributional detail

## 4. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** A representative five-country European experiment (n=4207) finds defaults produce medium-to-large effects on financial risk-taking, but also lower ratings of advisor honesty and advice quality when a default is used or refused, especially a one-size-fits-all default.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest EU evidence connecting default strength to perceived manipulativeness/trust cost, directly informing whether opt-out enrollment risks a dark-pattern perception.

**Why it matters here.** Direct EU evidence linking default strength to perceived manipulativeness/trust cost, especially when applied uniformly, exactly the reputational risk the brief flags for opt-out enrollment.

**Method.** Three experiments including a large representative multi-country European survey experiment on default framing in investment decisions.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management advice context with human advisor mediation, not a fully self-directed app
- no data on persistence or amount saved over time

## 5. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation default boxes on political campaign websites increased donations by over $43 million but also increased requested refunds by almost $3 million, with the largest impact on smaller, less-experienced donors who did not compensate by adjusting amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest evidence of a consumer digital default producing both a strong enrolment effect and a dark-pattern-like complaint/refund cost, directly bearing on the trade-off the brief is deciding.

**Why it matters here.** A genuine consumer, self-directed, digital-transaction default (not employer-mediated) showing both a large enrolment/behaviour effect and a refund/complaint signature that mirrors the support team's worry about passively-enrolled users disengaging or complaining.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design exploiting different rollout timing of default checkboxes across 2020 U.S. campaign websites.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a savings product, so amounts and stakes differ
- no measurement of outcomes beyond the immediate donation/refund window (no 6-12 month follow-up)

## 6. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 3/3

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrollment raised participation to over 85%, but about 80% of participants initially accepted the low default savings rate (2-3%) and conservative default fund, with half still at the default rate and fund after three years, offsetting wealth-accumulation gains from higher participation.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The classic empirical demonstration of anchoring on a preset contribution level, the specific mechanism the brief's Level decision turns on.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration that a preset contribution level anchors most enrollees for years, warning that whichever round-up multiplier we preselect will likely become the durable norm for most users, shaping which multiplier to choose.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms tracking savings behavior and asset allocation over multiple years under automatic enrollment.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) employer-plan setting, not a consumer app
- no publication year given; an older foundational study

## 7. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In a 220-participant RCT on voluntary pension saving in China, default-option and positive-narrative nudges significantly reduced willingness to contribute and the proportion of income saved, contrary to prevailing international evidence, with effects varying by income, education, and gender.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving and provides the distributional heterogeneity evidence the brief asked for.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest available contradiction of the 'defaults always help' premise the brief explicitly asked us to test against, plus heterogeneity by income/education/gender that speaks directly to who a default might harm.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey testing social-norm, framing, default-option, and narrative nudges on voluntary pension saving decisions.

**Limitations.**

- small online-survey sample (n=220) measuring stated willingness rather than real enrolment behaviour
- voluntary pension saving in China, a different cultural/regulatory context than an EU consumer banking app

## 8. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale field experiments at major UK banks show automatic enrolment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts reduces unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, worth an estimated £170-240 million market-wide annually, though alerts capture less than half of consumers' potential savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Nearest real-world analogue: UK consumer bank auto-enrolment into a financial-behaviour feature with quantified effect sizes at scale.

**Why it matters here.** The closest available evidence: a UK bank automatically enrolling retail customers into a consumer financial feature at scale, with quantified behavioural and monetary effects — this is the direct precedent for what auto-enrolling users into round-up savings could plausibly achieve and where its limits lie.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments run with UK retail banks on automatic enrolment into alert features.

**Limitations.**

- measures overdraft-alert behaviour, not a savings/round-up product specifically
- no explicit 6-12 month disengagement or withdrawal analysis reported in the abstract

## 9. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** Across three large firms, automatic enrollment raised 401(k) participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants stuck with the low default contribution rate (2-3%) and a conservative default fund, and even after three years half remained at the default — so the participation gain and the low default savings rate roughly offset in net asset accumulation, though the lower tail of the distribution still benefited.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The strongest available longitudinal evidence that a pre-set contribution level anchors participant choices for years, directly informing the round-up multiplier default-level decision.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantitative evidence that a pre-set contribution level anchors most users for years, directly bearing on whether our round-up multiplier default (1x/2x/5x) will anchor users at whatever level we pre-select, and a caution that a higher nominal enrolment rate can be offset by a low chosen level.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative panel data from three large U.S. firms before and after automatic-enrollment adoption.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) with an employer-selected default, not a consumer app
- three-firm sample from around 2001
- does not examine liquid-savings withdrawal or disengagement

## 10. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Using 41 million observations from Denmark, automatic employer contributions increased total wealth accumulation substantially, while subsidies requiring active choice raised saving by only about 1 cent per $1 of government cost, because roughly 85% of individuals are 'passive savers' unresponsive to subsidies but heavily influenced by automatic contributions.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Strongest available large-scale evidence on whether default-driven contributions change real savings amounts versus nominal enrolment via active choice, the core amount-vs-enrolment question the brief asks.

**Why it matters here.** The largest-scale, most rigorous evidence that defaults change real wealth accumulation for most people rather than just nominal enrolment, directly bearing on the support team's 'vanity enrolment' objection, even though the employer/government contribution mechanism differs from a self-directed app default.

**Method.** Large-scale administrative panel analysis of the Danish population comparing active-choice tax subsidies to passive/automatic retirement contributions.

**Limitations.**

- government/employer-administered retirement accounts, not a self-directed consumer banking app
- tests contribution-rate responses to subsidies, not opt-in/opt-out/active-choice enrolment mechanics directly
- Danish tax/administrative context, not EU consumer fintech
