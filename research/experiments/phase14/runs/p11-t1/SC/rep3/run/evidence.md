## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrinks the visibility of the credit-card minimum-payment autopay option cuts minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but does not significantly reduce debt, because nudged cardholders opt for autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum and some drop out of autopay entirely, raising missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest consumer-financial-product evidence that enrollment metrics can diverge sharply from the amount-level outcome the feature is meant to produce.

**Why it matters here.** This is direct evidence for the support team's 'vanity enrollment' worry in a genuinely consumer, self-directed financial product: a choice-architecture nudge changed nominal behavior without changing the substantive outcome (debt paid down), which is exactly the enrollment-vs-amount distinction the round-up feature decision turns on.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit card accounts with six-month follow-up, comparing nudged vs. control cardholders' autopay enrollment and payment behavior.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not savings accumulation
- nudge shrouded an option rather than setting a full opt-out default

## 2. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults on 2020 US campaign websites increased donations by over $43 million but also increased requested refunds by almost $3 million, with the largest impact on smaller, less-experienced donors who likely did not intend a recurring commitment.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the 'defaults always help' premise with a large quantified estimate of both benefit and consumer detriment, plus the exact heterogeneity (inexperienced, small users) the brief asks about.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world evidence that a pre-checked recurring-contribution default can lift nominal sign-up while simultaneously generating regret and complaints in the least-experienced users — precisely the support team's objection, quantified.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across multiple political campaign websites that added pre-checked recurring-donation boxes at different times; observational/quasi-experimental.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a savings product
- refund requests are a proxy for regret, not a direct complaint or withdrawal metric

## 3. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for when and how default effects persist or fade downstream, identifying time course of choice/consumption, antecedent preferences, and trade-off salience as the key moderators of long-run default effects.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). The review-level synthesis the brief asks for on whether and how default effects persist versus decay, in a genuinely consumer (non-employer) framing.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the project a structured way to predict which of our two design choices (entry, level) is likely to show a lasting vs. fading effect, and why enrolled-but-disengaged users are a documented, theorized pattern rather than an anecdote from support.

**Method.** Conceptual review and framework synthesis of the consumer-default literature; no new primary data.

**Limitations.**

- conceptual synthesis, not a quantitative meta-analysis
- no numeric effect sizes to anchor design decisions
- not specific to financial products

## 4. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a representative five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges shift risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes, but implementing a default (especially one people refuse) lowers users' rating of the advice/wealth manager, an effect mediated by perceived honesty; question framing in the default presentation also shifts chosen allocations.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The only EU-context experimental evidence quantifying when a default is perceived as manipulative, and how framing of the default level shifts chosen amounts — bears directly on both the entry and level decisions.

**Why it matters here.** Directly quantifies the EU-consumer-relevant reputational cost of defaults — a default that people override actively damages trust — which bears squarely on whether an opt-out round-up default will read as helpful or manipulative, and on how the preset multiplier should be framed to avoid an anchoring/dark-pattern read.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, including a large representative multi-country European sample, manipulating default presence and framing of investment defaults.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management investment-allocation context, not a round-up savings app
- advisor-mediated setting differs somewhat from a purely self-service app flow

## 5. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6 percentage points from automatic enrollment and 0.3 points from default auto-escalation once job separation, unvested-balance withdrawal, and auto-escalation opt-out are accounted for; only 40% of those defaulted into auto-escalation follow through on the first scheduled increase.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving amounts, which the brief explicitly asked us to test.

**Why it matters here.** The single strongest quantified rebuttal to the 'defaults will lift enrolment, so default everyone in' argument: it shows the true steady-state effect of automatic enrollment/escalation is far smaller than headline participation-rate lifts suggest, directly undercutting the internal case for full opt-out enrollment.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating employee turnover, withdrawal-on-separation, and auto-escalation opt-out dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- employer-plan mechanics (vesting, job separation) don't map onto a banking app

## 6. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale field experiments at major UK banks show automatic enrollment into just-in-time text alerts reduced unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in annual market-wide savings, though alerts captured less than half of potential avoidable savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly on-point: UK retail-bank consumer product, opt-out/auto-enrollment mechanism, quantified real behavioral and monetary effects, not an employer-mediated setting.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world precedent for opt-out enrollment inside an existing consumer banking app: it quantifies both the size of the behavior-change benefit and its shortfall relative to full potential, which is exactly the trade-off the enrolment-default decision needs measured against.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments across major UK retail banks; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- overdraft alerts, not a savings/round-up feature
- no explicit 6-12 month persistence breakdown given in the abstract

## 8. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In a 220-participant RCT on voluntary pension saving in China, all tested nudges — including default options — significantly reduced willingness to contribute relative to a control group, with default-option and positive-narrative nudges producing the largest backfire, and effects varying by income, education, gender, and financial motivation.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest contradicting evidence in this batch that a default can actively reduce rather than raise contribution intentions, satisfying the brief's explicit request for a contradicting paper.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving: here the default option nudge backfires and lowers willingness to contribute, with heterogeneity by demographic group that mirrors the brief's distributional concern.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey testing social-norm, framing, default-option, and narrative nudges on voluntary pension contribution decisions; N=220.

**Limitations.**

- hypothetical/survey-based willingness rather than real enrollment behavior
- small sample (N=220) and a different cultural/regulatory context (China, not EU)
- pension savings context rather than liquid round-up savings

## 10. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Using 41 million Danish observations, each $1 of tax-subsidy expenditure raised total saving by only 1 cent because 85% of people are 'passive savers' unmoved by subsidies but heavily influenced by automatic contributions, while the 15% who are 'active' mainly shift assets across accounts rather than saving more.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Uniquely detailed evidence distinguishing nominal enrolment from real savings impact across active/passive saver types, the closest existing answer to the brief's central 'vanity metric' question.

**Why it matters here.** The strongest available evidence that a default changes real accumulated wealth for the majority who are passive, not merely who is nominally enrolled — directly answers the brief's question of whether defaults change the amount saved versus just enrolment counts, and shows the effect is concentrated among the financially less sophisticated, informing who is helped.

**Method.** Large-scale administrative panel data analysis (Denmark, ~41 million observations) exploiting variation between active-choice subsidy policies and automatic/passive contribution policies.

**Limitations.**

- government retirement/tax-advantaged accounts in Denmark, not a self-directed liquid consumer app
- the employer/state-as-intermediary trust mechanism the brief flags as non-transferable may partly drive the passive-saver result
- no data on withdrawal or disengagement of passive savers
