## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouded the credit-card minimum-payment autopay option cut minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce debt, and actually reduced autopay enrollment and increased missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Consumer, self-directed, liquid financial-product field experiment that contradicts the premise that shifting defaults reliably improves the outcome people care about.

**Why it matters here.** Direct consumer-financial evidence that changing enrollment defaults can shift nominal enrollment without improving the underlying financial outcome, and can even backfire via missed payments — the exact 'vanity enrollment' risk the support team is flagging for round-up defaults.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit-card accounts with six-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- credit-card debt paydown, not savings accumulation
- does not test opt-out vs active-choice enrollment structures directly

## 2. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrollment and default auto-escalation: steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of auto-escalation defaults survive the first escalation date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that defaults durably raise saving, with real effect sizes at multiple horizons.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of our support team's objection: default-driven enrollment gains shrink sharply once turnover, withdrawal, and later opt-out are accounted for — exactly the persistence question the brief asks about.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans accounting for job separation, balance withdrawal, and opt-out from auto-escalation.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated context differs from a self-service banking app

## 3. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments (including n=4207 across five European countries), default nudges shift risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes, but they also lower how trustworthy/honest the advice is perceived to be, especially when the targeted individual refuses the default; framing of the default presentation (e.g., asking amount to invest vs. amount to keep safe) produces smaller but real anchoring shifts.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). European, consumer-financial experimental evidence on anchoring by presented defaults and on when a default is perceived as manipulative — squarely the questions behind the level and entry-mode decisions.

**Why it matters here.** Directly informs both open decisions: it shows preset defaults anchor chosen amounts, and quantifies the reputational/manipulation cost of an unwanted default being applied — the EU dark-pattern risk the brief flags for choosing among opt-in/opt-out/active-choice designs.

**Method.** Three experiments including a large representative multi-country European sample; between-subjects manipulation of default presence and framing.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-manager advisory context rather than a purely self-service app
- risk-allocation decisions, not round-up multipliers specifically

## 4. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** In 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions, 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out effort cost together — removing either friction alone does not sustain adherence — and adding an intermediate preset option (15% alongside 20/25/30%) captures 69% of the achievable consumer welfare gain from full menu redesign.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Names an explicit transferable method (deviation-cost vs. opt-out-cost decomposition and preset-menu design) for structuring the multiplier-level decision, in a consumer self-directed opt-out setting.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a transferable method for the multiplier-level decision: it separates 'people pick the preset because changing it is annoying' from 'people pick the preset because it matches their preference,' and shows how adding one well-chosen extra preset level can capture most of the welfare gain — directly usable for deciding between a 1x/2x/5x menu and an empty field.

**Method.** Structural model estimated on 8.6M transaction records plus an independent stated-preference survey identifying true preferences.

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions, not a savings product
- one-off payment context, not a persistent enrollment/withdrawal decision

## 5. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for when defaults do or do not produce lasting ('downstream') behavior change, identifying time course, prior preferences, and trade-off salience as key moderators.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). General consumer choice-architecture synthesis on persistence of default effects, matching the brief's central open question about whether enrollment lift survives past onboarding.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a framework for predicting when our opt-out default will or won't persist past sign-up — directly shapes what to measure (retention, disengagement) and how to interpret it, rather than dictating the design itself.

**Method.** Conceptual synthesis and research agenda, abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical/synthesis paper, not a new empirical estimate
- not specific to financial products

## 6. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment pushed 401(k) participation above 85%, but roughly 80% of participants accepted both the default savings rate (2-3%) and default conservative fund, with half still at the default after three years, largely offsetting participation gains against low default anchoring.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The foundational anchoring-on-default-level study the newer literature responds to; directly relevant to the level decision despite being off-domain for the consumer-app setting.

**Why it matters here.** The classic demonstration that a pre-set default level anchors most enrollees onto it for years — the strongest historical evidence for why choosing which multiplier (1x/2x/5x) to pre-select matters as much as the entry mode.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms tracked over multiple years.

**Limitations.**

- pre-2010s employer 401(k) data, predates app-based fintech and EU consumer-protection climate
- employer-mediated trust context does not transfer

## 7. The Behavioral Foundations of Default Effects: Theory and Evidence from Medicare Part D

Zarek Brot-Goldberg, Timothy Layton, Boris Vabson, Adelina Yanyue Wang · 2023 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210013>

**Key finding.** Default rules in Medicare Part D have large, persistent effects on enrollment and drug use, and beneficiary passivity is extremely insensitive to the value of the default option, even with random assignment.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Rigorous individual-choice default study with an active-choice comparison, useful analogy for how sticky opt-out defaults are even against high stakes.

**Why it matters here.** Strong causal evidence that passivity toward defaults is not simply low engagement but structural inattention — relevant to interpreting whether opt-out enrollment reflects genuine preference or inertia in our product.

**Method.** Three natural experiments including random assignment to default options in a large administrative dataset.

**Limitations.**

- health-insurance/drug-plan setting, not financial savings
- low-income beneficiary population differs from a general banking-app user base

## 8. Default Options and Retirement Saving Dynamics

Taha Choukhmane · 2025 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210881>

**Key finding.** Auto-enrollment's short-run (12-month) gains in participation and contributions attenuate substantially by 36 months, though it does reduce inequality in savings; a life-cycle model fits this with a switching cost of about $250.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly measures the persistence-of-default-effect question central to the brief, with a multi-horizon comparison.

**Why it matters here.** Quantifies exactly how much the default 'enrollment win' fades over 1–3 years, giving a concrete benchmark for judging whether an opt-out round-up feature's enrollment numbers will hold up at 6–12 months.

**Method.** Panel data from over 100 US retirement plans plus a UK representative panel, combined with a structural life-cycle consumption-savings model.

**Limitations.**

- employer-mediated retirement plans, not a consumer banking app
- liquidity and stakes differ greatly from small round-up sums

## 9. Behavioral Household Finance

John Beshears, James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian · 2018 · National Bureau of Economic Research · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w24854>

**Key finding.** A broad overview of household financial behavior and the interventions (education, peer effects, product design, choice architecture, and price/quantity nudges) firms and governments use to shape it, synthesizing where behavioral patterns deviate from the classical model.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). A high-quality, consumer-setting (not employer-plan) synthesis of behavioral household finance that covers defaults, choice architecture, and product design — the exact intervention category the brief is deciding on.

**Why it matters here.** Frames the whole choice-architecture toolkit (including defaults and product design) in a consumer household-finance setting rather than an employer-plan one, giving the team a map of which levers (not just opt-out defaults) are evidenced to matter for self-directed financial decisions like the round-up feature.

**Method.** Narrative literature review/book chapter covering consumption-savings, borrowing, payments, asset allocation, and insurance; abstract-only, no effect sizes given.

**Limitations.**

- broad survey rather than a focused study of enrollment defaults
- no quantitative effect sizes in the abstract
- does not isolate active-choice vs opt-out vs opt-in comparisons specifically

## 10. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Field experiments show that 'enhanced active choice' — active choice framed to highlight the losses of the non-preferred option — can motivate behavior change and is proposed as a complement to, or substitute for, automatic enrollment.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). Introduces and field-tests 'enhanced active choice' as an explicit alternative to opt-out enrollment, the exact entry-design technique the brief is deciding between, with an explicit link to savings/opt-out policy contexts.

**Why it matters here.** Directly speaks to the entry-design decision: it offers a tested, specific variant of active choice (loss-framed) that could outperform plain active choice or opt-out without the passive-enrollment disengagement risk the support team worries about — a concrete design option worth prototyping for onboarding.

**Method.** Series of studies including two field experiments testing active choice and a novel 'enhanced active choice' variant; abstract-only, no effect sizes given.

**Limitations.**

- setting is medication adherence/health decisions, not consumer financial products
- no quantitative effect sizes reported in the abstract
- does not measure amount saved or persistence over months
