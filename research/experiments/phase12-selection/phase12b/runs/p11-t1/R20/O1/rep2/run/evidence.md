## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the autopay-minimum option reduced minimum-only payments by 23% at six months but did not reduce credit card debt, and it lowered autopay enrollment while increasing missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Directly tests whether a choice-architecture nudge that raises a headline behavioral metric also improves the substantive outcome, the crux of the brief's enrollment-vs-vanity trade-off.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a nudge can move an enrollment-like behavioral metric without moving the real financial outcome, and can create new harms — the exact 'vanity enrollment' risk the support team is warning about for round-up sign-ups.

**Method.** Field experiment (nudge in autopay enrollment default) on real credit-card accounts, six-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- Credit-card debt paydown context, not a savings product
- no measure of amount saved or persistence beyond six months

## 2. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Across nine 401(k) plans, automatic enrollment raises steady-state savings by only 0.6% of income and default auto-escalation by 0.3%, because employees leave firms, withdraw balances, and opt out of escalation.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving amounts, which the brief asked us to test against.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest statement of our support team's objection: the enrolment lift is real at signup and much smaller later once withdrawal and opt-out are accounted for.

**Method.** Analysis of administrative data from nine 401(k) plans incorporating turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- Retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- abstract gives income-share effects rather than app-comparable enrolment/engagement rates

## 3. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across experiments including a 4,207-person five-country European study, default nudges produce medium-to-large effects on investment risk-taking, but also lower ratings of the wealth manager's advice — especially when the targeted default is refused — mediated by perceived honesty.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). European consumer-finance evidence on when a default reads as manipulative rather than helpful, exactly the dark-pattern risk the brief flags.

**Why it matters here.** Directly addresses the EU reputational-risk question in the brief: a default that shapes behavior can simultaneously damage users' trust in the institution, especially when rejected — a caution for how the round-up default and its exit flow should be framed.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative multi-country European experiment on default framing in investment advice.

**Limitations.**

- Wealth-management investment context rather than a round-up savings pot
- no persistence data beyond the experimental session

## 4. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · computational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model of 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions finds 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out cost, and adding a lower preset menu option captures 69% of the achievable welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Explicit method transfer: its decomposition of default adherence into deviation-cost and opt-out-cost frictions, and its counterfactual of adding a lower menu option, is a directly applicable technique for designing the round-up multiplier menu.

**Why it matters here.** The friction-decomposition method and the counterfactual of adding a lower preset menu tier transfer directly to designing the round-up multiplier menu: it shows how a lower tier can recover most of the welfare loss from anchoring without abandoning presets.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on transaction data plus an independent stated-preference survey, with counterfactual menu-design simulations.

**Limitations.**

- Tipping decisions at point-of-sale, not a savings product
- single-city taxi population, not banking-app users

## 5. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for downstream (later) effects of default choice architecture, identifying time course of choice, antecedent preferences, and trade-off salience as key moderators of whether default effects persist.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). A recent, consumer-focused theoretical synthesis of exactly the persistence and heterogeneity questions the brief asks about.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a general, consumer-behavior (not employer-plan) framework for predicting when a default's initial enrollment effect will or will not persist and who it will affect differently — useful for designing the 6-12 month measurement plan.

**Method.** Theoretical review and research-agenda paper synthesizing prior consumer-behavior findings, abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- Conceptual/theoretical, no new effect sizes
- not specific to financial products

## 6. Regulating Dark Patterns

Martin Brenncke · 2023 · Aston Publications Explorer (Aston University) · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2310.00340>

**Key finding.** Maps EU dark-pattern law onto a six-category taxonomy of autonomy violations, framing pre-selected defaults and similar choice-architecture practices as regulable when they undermine informed consumer decision-making.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C5 distributional and manipulation risk (3/3). Gives the EU-specific manipulation/dark-pattern framework the brief explicitly asks for when weighing opt-out against active choice.

**Why it matters here.** Directly bears on the entry-design decision: it tells us where the line sits between a helpful opt-out default and a regulable dark pattern under EU law, which is the reputational and legal risk the brief flags for opt-out enrolment.

**Method.** Doctrinal/normative legal analysis of EU consumer-protection law; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- legal/normative analysis, not empirical behavioural evidence
- no financial-product-specific case studies or effect sizes
- EU law framework, not a test of any particular default design

## 7. Automatic Enrollment with a 12% Default Contribution Rate

John Beshears, Ruofei Guo, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31601>

**Key finding.** With a very high 12% default contribution rate, 25% of employees still had not opted out after 12 months (vs. ~50% opt-out under lower defaults elsewhere), and many stayed anchored at 12% even though it was a suboptimal, unmatched contribution rate, especially lower-income employees.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Quantifies default anchoring and shows distributional harm to less-sophisticated savers, both core sub-questions of the brief.

**Why it matters here.** Strong quantitative evidence that a pre-set default anchors people at a level many would not have chosen and that this anchoring effect is stronger for lower-income (less sophisticated) users — directly relevant to how we choose the pre-selected multiplier and to the brief's who's-helped/who's-hurt question.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from one employer's retirement plan with a 12% default; compared against a literature review of lower-default opt-out rates.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored retirement plan, not a consumer self-directed app
- no EU regulatory context
- single-employer, 12-month horizon only

## 8. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** At three large firms, automatic 401(k) enrollment pushed participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants initially accepted the low default contribution rate and fund, and half remained at the default rate/fund after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The classic evidence for level-anchoring on pre-set defaults, the exact mechanism the brief worries about for the round-up multiplier choice.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration that a pre-set default level strongly anchors the level people end up at for years, directly bearing on whether a pre-selected round-up multiplier will anchor most users below a higher tier.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms' 401(k) plans.

**Limitations.**

- Employer-sponsored 401(k), not a consumer app
- predates the fintech/liquidity/regulatory context the brief says has changed

## 9. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Introduces and field-tests 'enhanced active choice' — active choice that highlights the losses of the non-preferred option — as an alternative to automatic enrollment when a straight default is infeasible or ethically fraught, explicitly proposed as a complement to automatic enrollment in savings contexts.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). Directly addresses active choice vs. defaults and proposes a specific enhanced-active-choice mechanism the brief's onboarding decision could adopt.

**Why it matters here.** This is the direct evidence base for the third onboarding option the brief is weighing — whether active choice captures most of the enrolment benefit without the opt-out disengagement cost — and it offers a concrete design variant (loss-framed active choice) the team could implement instead of a plain forced choice.

**Method.** Review of prior active-choice literature plus a series of studies including two field experiments; abstract-only for effect sizes.

**Limitations.**

- primary experiments are in medication-adherence, not financial, settings
- abstract gives no quantitative effect sizes for the savings analogy
- does not report persistence/withdrawal outcomes

## 10. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrollment raised participation above 85% at three firms, but ~80% of participants initially accepted the low default savings rate (2-3%) and conservative default fund, and half were still at the default rate and fund after three years, roughly offsetting the participation gain in aggregate wealth accumulation.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The foundational study on default persistence and anchoring that the newer, more relevant papers in this set are responding to.

**Why it matters here.** The classic demonstration that defaults raise nominal enrolment but strongly anchor the contribution level and persist for years — exactly the anchoring-versus-vanity-enrolment tension the brief wants tested, giving the benchmark persistence figures (half still at default after 3 years).

**Method.** Multi-year administrative panel data from three large firms with automatic 401(k) enrollment.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored retirement plan, pre-dates modern fintech/app context
- no EU regulatory framing
- locked, illiquid savings vehicle unlike round-up sums
