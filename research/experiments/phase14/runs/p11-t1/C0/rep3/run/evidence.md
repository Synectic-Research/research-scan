## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** Shrouding the autopay-minimum option reduced minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, since nudged users mostly chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum, and missed payments increased.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Direct consumer financial product evidence that a default/nudge can shift enrollment-like metrics without moving the outcome that matters, speaking to the brief's amount-vs-nominal-enrollment question.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a nudge intervention can move an enrollment-adjacent metric while leaving the substantive outcome unchanged and even worsening some behaviors — direct support for the team's concern that headline enrollment could be a vanity metric for round-up savings.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit card accounts with a six-month follow-up on payment behavior.

**Limitations.**

- Studies credit-card minimum-payment nudging, not a savings round-up feature
- Only 6-month follow-up, not 12 months
- Mechanism (shrouding an option) differs from opt-in/opt-out/active-choice framing

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** A default nudge shifted investment risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes across five European countries, but lowered ratings of the advice given compared to no default, especially when the default was refused, with perceived advisor honesty mediating the effect.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer financial-decision experiment addressing exactly when a default is perceived as manipulative, squarely the dark-pattern/reputational risk the brief flags.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests when a financial default reads as manipulative to European consumers, informing how the round-up entry screen should be framed to avoid a dark-pattern perception while still capturing enrollment.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative five-country European sample (n=4207), testing default nudges and framing of default presentation in investment decisions.

**Limitations.**

- Wealth-management/investment risk context rather than round-up savings
- Effect on longer-term behavior not reported, only immediate choice and advice rating
- Abstract gives effect sizes qualitatively (medium-to-large), not exact numbers

## 3. The Effect of Choice Screens on Mobile Browser Usage: Evidence from the EU Digital Markets Act [UNVERIFIED — check manually]

Jesper Akesson, Kush Amlani, Raul Cepeda Suarez, Emily Chissell et al. · 2026 · Social Science Research Network · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** Following the EU Digital Markets Act's mandated browser choice screens, Firefox usage on iOS rose 113% and on Android 12% relative to a no-mandate counterfactual, with effects still present 15 months after rollout.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). The most directly relevant recent evidence on active/forced choice versus defaults in a mobile app context under EU regulation, with unusually long follow-up.

**Why it matters here.** Direct, long-horizon (15-month) evidence that an EU-mandated active-choice screen produces large, persistent behaviour change without a default — the clearest real-world test of whether active choice captures meaningful benefit versus a default, exactly what the entry-mechanism decision needs.

**Method.** Difference-in-differences design exploiting the staggered rollout of DMA-mandated choice screens on iOS and Android.

**Limitations.**

- mobile browser choice, not a financial enrolment or savings decision
- platform-level usage share, not individual engagement or amount outcomes

## 5. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment raises steady-state saving rates by only 0.6% of income and default auto-escalation by only 0.3%, as job turnover, pre-vesting withdrawals, and opt-outs (only 40% escalate on the first date) erode the headline effect.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving amounts, directly answering the brief's amount-vs-nominal-enrollment question.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantitative statement of the support team's objection: the enrollment lift from a default is real at signup but much smaller in steady state once turnover and opt-out are accounted for.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- Retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- Nine firm-level 401(k) plans, may not generalize

## 6. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults raised campaign donations by over $43M but also increased requested refunds by almost $3M, with larger effects on smaller and first-time donors who likely did not intend the recurring commitment.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly evidences the trade-off the brief asks about: a default that increases nominal enrolment/amount while also increasing complaint-like behaviour, with clear heterogeneity by donor experience and size.

**Why it matters here.** Real-world evidence that a recurring-contribution default both lifts nominal sign-up and generates unwanted commitments and refund/complaint behaviour, mirroring exactly the support team's concern about passively-enrolled users regretting the round-up feature.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences causal design across political campaign websites that added pre-checked recurring-donation boxes at different times.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a savings product
- measures refunds as a complaint proxy rather than direct satisfaction or withdrawal data

## 7. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** In 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions, 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out cost together; removing either friction alone would not sustain adherence, and adding a lower tip-menu option captures 69% of the achievable welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). The friction-decomposition method (deviation cost vs. opt-out cost) is an explicit, transferable technique for designing preset-menu defaults and predicting anchoring on the round-up multiplier.

**Why it matters here.** Decomposes exactly why a preset menu option anchors choice into two separable, manipulable frictions — a technique directly transferable to designing the round-up multiplier menu and calibrating how easy opting out should be, and it shows adding an intermediate option can raise welfare cheaply.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on transaction data plus an independent stated-preference survey of consumer preferences.

**Limitations.**

- Setting is taxi tipping menus, not financial savings
- One-time transactional choice rather than a recurring savings decision
- US urban context, not EU

## 8. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrolment into just-in-time text alerts cuts unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, though this captures less than half of avoidable savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Consumer-financial, self-directed, bank-app setting with a real automatic-enrolment default and measured downstream financial outcomes — the closest analogue to the brief's own product.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world precedent for auto-enrolling bank customers (not employees) into a beneficial default feature and measuring genuine behavioural savings, directly supporting the case for opt-out enrolment in a banking app.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments at major UK banks measuring real transaction outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- studies alert/notification enrolment rather than a savings pot enrolment or contribution-level decision
- no anchoring or multi-year persistence data given

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A randomized nudge (payment reminders) in a Brazilian financial-management app cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised overdraft fees by 9%, with users who already had overdraft histories experiencing a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Provides rare experimental evidence of a nudge with heterogeneous, sometimes harmful, effects in a consumer fintech setting, speaking directly to the brief's 'who is helped and who is hurt' question.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the brief's concern about heterogeneous harm from choice-architecture interventions: a nudge that helps most users on average can net-harm a vulnerable subgroup, which is exactly the distributional check needed before defaulting everyone into round-up savings.

**Method.** Field experiment (RCT) with a financial management platform in Brazil, comparing users randomized to receive payment reminders versus not, tracking fee outcomes across credit card and checking accounts.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrollment defaults or contribution-level presets
- credit card/overdraft fee context rather than a savings sweep product
- no data on multi-month enrollment persistence or contribution amounts
