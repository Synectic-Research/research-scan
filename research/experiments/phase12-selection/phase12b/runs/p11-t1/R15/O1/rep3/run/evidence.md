## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment nudge shrouding the minimum-payment autopay option cut minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because nudged cardholders chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Field RCT on consumer financial defaults showing enrollment change without behavioral improvement, directly informing the entry-mechanism and 'vanity metric' concern.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the 'defaults always help' premise in a comparable consumer financial product: shifting enrollment behavior did not change the underlying financial outcome, warning that our enrollment lift may not translate into more savings.

**Method.** Field RCT on credit cardholders with six-month follow-up on autopay enrollment, payment amounts, and debt.

**Limitations.**

- studies credit card debt paydown, not savings enrollment
- does not test preset multiplier anchoring directly

## 2. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine automatic enrollment and auto-escalation effects: steady-state savings rates rise only 0.6% and 0.3% of income respectively, and only 40% of employees complete their first auto-escalation before opting out.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly quantifies how much smaller default effects are over the medium/long run than initial estimates suggest, matching the brief's core persistence question.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of the support team's objection: the enrollment lift from defaults shrinks sharply once turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out are accounted for, directly bearing on whether our enrollment numbers will be vanity.

**Method.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job turnover, withdrawal upon separation, and auto-escalation opt-out over the medium/long run.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement accounts, not liquid consumer round-up savings
- employer-plan turnover dynamics don't map onto an app-based product

## 3. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Default nudges have medium-to-large effects on consumer risk-taking, but a default nudge (especially one people refuse) lowers ratings of the wealth manager's honesty and advice quality, with framing of the ask also shifting invested amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly measures manipulation perception and framing effects of defaults among EU consumers in a financial-advice context, matching the brief's dark-pattern and framing concerns.

**Why it matters here.** Provides EU consumer evidence that defaults can damage trust and perceived honesty — central to the dark-pattern risk the brief flags for the entry-mechanism decision — and shows framing of the contribution ask shifts amounts, relevant to how the multiplier field is worded.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative five-European-country sample (n=4207), on default-nudged financial investment decisions.

**Limitations.**

- financial-advisor context rather than a self-serve app
- risk-taking investment decisions, not round-up savings specifically

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

## 5. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model of 8.6 million tipping decisions finds 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of deviation cost and opt-out cost together, and a counterfactual added preset (15% alongside 20/25/30) captures 69% of achievable welfare gains at 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Explicit method transfer: its structural friction-decomposition and counterfactual preset-menu analysis is directly applicable to choosing and testing which multiplier to pre-select.

**Why it matters here.** The deviation-cost/opt-out-cost decomposition and counterfactual preset-menu method is the specific technique for deciding which round-up multiplier value to pre-select and how many preset options to offer, transferring directly to the level-design decision.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on NYC taxi transaction data plus a stated-preference survey, with counterfactual menu simulations.

**Limitations.**

- taxi tipping, not financial savings
- no data on longer-term persistence of chosen behavior

## 6. The Behavioral Foundations of Default Effects: Theory and Evidence from Medicare Part D

Zarek Brot-Goldberg, Timothy Layton, Boris Vabson, Adelina Yanyue Wang · 2023 · American Economic Review · experimental · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210013>

**Key finding.** Default rules in Medicare Part D have large, persistent effects on enrollment and drug utilization, and beneficiary passivity is highly insensitive even to substantial losses from the default option.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Strong evidence on persistence and insensitivity of passive default behavior, with distributional detail on low-income beneficiaries, relevant by analogy to sticky opt-out defaults.

**Why it matters here.** Shows default stickiness can persist even when it hurts the enrollee, cautioning against assuming an opt-out default is automatically welfare-improving for disengaged consumers.

**Method.** Three natural/randomized experiments using random assignment to default options among Medicare beneficiaries.

**Limitations.**

- health insurance/drug benefit setting, not a financial savings product
- low-income Medicare population differs from a general banking app user base

## 8. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic 401(k) enrollment pushed participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants anchored on the low default contribution rate (2-3%) and conservative default fund, with half still at the default after three years.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Foundational study establishing that preset default levels anchor participant choices persistently, the classic evidence the anchoring literature builds on.

**Why it matters here.** The classic demonstration that a pre-set default level strongly anchors the chosen contribution amount for years — the foundational evidence behind the brief's anchoring concern for the round-up multiplier.

**Method.** Administrative data analysis across three large firms tracking 401(k) participation and default anchoring over three years.

**Limitations.**

- employer 401(k) plan, not a consumer app
- old, foundational study predating current fintech/regulatory context

## 9. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Using 41 million Danish observations, passive automatic employer contributions raised savings substantially while active-choice tax subsidies mostly shifted assets between accounts (1 cent of new saving per $1 of subsidy); ~85% of individuals are 'passive savers' unresponsive to subsidies but strongly moved by automatic defaults, while active savers are wealthier and more sophisticated.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Foundational distributional evidence on who benefits from passive defaults versus active-choice policies, directly relevant to the brief's equity concern.

**Why it matters here.** The classic evidence that passive defaults reach financially unsophisticated savers that active-choice policies miss — central to the brief's 'who is helped, who is hurt' question when weighing active choice against opt-out.

**Method.** Population-level administrative panel data from Denmark comparing responses to active-choice subsidies versus passive automatic contributions.

**Limitations.**

- Danish retirement/taxable savings accounts, not a consumer app
- outside the recency window the brief prioritizes

## 10. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · other · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Proposes and tests 'enhanced active choice' — a variant of active choice that highlights losses from the non-preferred option — as an alternative to automatic enrollment when defaults are infeasible or unethical, via field experiments in medication adherence.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). One of the foundational papers introducing and testing active choice as a named alternative to opt-in/opt-out defaults, directly bearing on the entry-mechanism decision even though the domain differs.

**Why it matters here.** Directly informs the active-choice option for the entry decision: shows active choice can be strengthened by loss-framing rather than treated as a weaker compromise between opt-in and opt-out, which matters if the team wants active choice to capture more of the opt-out enrolment lift.

**Method.** Series of studies including two field experiments testing active choice and enhanced active choice against standard framing; abstract-only, health-care/medication-adherence context.

**Limitations.**

- setting is medication adherence, not consumer financial products
- no effect sizes for savings-specific behavior
- field experiments are in health care, not fintech
