## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** Shrouding the autopay-minimum option decreased minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, since nudged cardholders set autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly tests a defaults/nudge intervention in a consumer financial app with liquidity-driven heterogeneity, closely analogous to the round-up savings design.

**Why it matters here.** Shows that a choice-architecture nudge in a consumer financial product can shift a passive enrollment metric without improving the underlying financial outcome, and can even backfire for liquidity-constrained users — a direct warning against treating enrollment lift as the success metric.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit cards testing an autopay-shrouding nudge, with six-month outcome measurement.

**Limitations.**

- Domain is credit-card repayment, not savings
- No measurement beyond six months

## 2. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrollment and of default contribution auto-escalation; steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that defaults durably raise saving, providing the counter-evidence the brief explicitly asked for.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest statement of our support team's objection: the enrolment lift is real at signup and much smaller once medium-run attrition and opt-out are accounted for.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job separation, withdrawal at separation, and auto-escalation opt-out. Abstract-only for further detail.

**Limitations.**

- Retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- Focuses on job-separation dynamics specific to employer plans

## 3. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Default nudges produce medium-to-large effects on financial risk-taking, but recipients rate wealth-manager advice lower when a default is used (and lower still when they refuse it), an effect mediated by perceived advisor honesty.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly quantifies the reputational/manipulation cost of defaults in an EU consumer financial-advice setting, the closest evidence to the brief's dark-pattern concern.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests when a financial default reads as manipulative versus helpful in a consumer-facing EU context — the exact reputational risk the brief flags — and argues for individualized defaults over one-size-fits-all, bearing directly on both the entry and level design decisions.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative-sample experiment across five European countries (n=4207).

**Limitations.**

- Wealth-management advice setting rather than a mobile banking app
- No long-term persistence data

## 4. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and conceptual framework for when and how default choice-architecture effects persist or fade downstream, shaped by the time course of choice/consumption, antecedent preferences, and trade-off salience.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Directly addresses the persistence question with a general consumer-behavior framework applicable beyond employer plans.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the project a structured way to predict and measure whether the round-up default's enrollment effect will persist or decay, rather than assuming persistence by default.

**Method.** Conceptual review and framework synthesis of the default-effects literature. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- Conceptual/theoretical, not an empirical estimate
- Not specific to financial products

## 5. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** Default adherence decomposes into deviation cost and opt-out cost, both jointly necessary (84% of adherence from their interaction); adding an intermediate 15% preset tip option below the existing menu captures 69% of achievable welfare gain at only 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Closest available quantitative evidence on how changing a preset menu of default levels shifts consumer choice and welfare, directly analogous to the round-up multiplier decision.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a rigorous, quantified account of how much of a default's grip on behavior is preference-driven versus friction-driven, and shows empirically how changing a menu of preset levels reallocates behavior and welfare — directly informs the preset multiplier design decision.

**Method.** Structural model estimated on 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping transactions, validated against a stated-preference survey.

**Limitations.**

- Domain is tipping, not savings or financial products
- Single-shot decision rather than a recurring enrollment/contribution choice

## 7. Default Options and Retirement Saving Dynamics

Taha Choukhmane · 2025 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210881>

**Key finding.** Auto-enrollment's participation and contribution gains attenuate substantially between 12 and 36 months, though it still reduces savings inequality; a life-cycle model fits this pattern with a switching cost of about $250.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best available quantification of medium-term attenuation of default enrollment gains, central to the persistence question despite the retirement-plan setting.

**Why it matters here.** Directly quantifies how much of the enrollment/contribution gain from a default fades by medium-term horizons, speaking to the brief's core question about whether the effect persists at 6-12 months and beyond.

**Method.** Panel analysis of over 100 US retirement plans and a representative UK panel, plus a structural life-cycle consumption-savings model.

**Limitations.**

- Retirement-plan setting rather than liquid consumer savings
- Employer/national-system-mediated enrollment, not self-directed app

## 8. Perspectives on the Economics of Aging

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · n.d. · RePEc: Research Papers in Economics · observational · overall 2/3

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment raised 401(k) participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants initially accepted both the default contribution rate and default fund, and half still did so after three years — clear anchoring on the default.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The classic evidence for anchoring on a preset default level, directly relevant to whether a pre-set round-up multiplier will anchor choice.

**Why it matters here.** The foundational demonstration of anchoring on preset default levels that all subsequent 'does the preset level distort choice' work argues with — sets the baseline the pre-set multiplier decision should be measured against.

**Method.** Administrative panel data from three large firms on 401(k) automatic enrollment, tracked over several years.

**Limitations.**

- Employer 401(k) setting rather than a consumer app
- Predates modern digital/liquid-savings and EU regulatory context

## 9. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Introduces and field-tests 'enhanced active choice' — a forced-choice design that highlights losses in the non-preferred option — as an alternative to automatic (opt-out) enrollment when defaults are infeasible or ethically fraught.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). Directly proposes and tests the active-choice/enhanced-active-choice mechanism that is one of the three entry options the brief is choosing between, with an explicit note that opt-out policies originated in savings behavior.

**Why it matters here.** This is the clearest test of exactly the 'active choice' option the brief is deciding between for feature entry, and its enhanced variant (highlighting the loss from not saving) is a directly buildable onboarding-screen design for the round-up feature.

**Method.** Series of studies including two field experiments testing active choice and a novel loss-framed variant against standard opt-out/default designs; abstract-only detail on effect sizes.

**Limitations.**

- conducted in medication-adherence field experiments, not a consumer financial product
- no persistence/6-month outcome data given
- abstract does not quantify effect sizes

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w24854>
