## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding a minimum-payment autopay default cut minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because nudged users anchored autopay amounts just above the minimum and manual payments fell.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Consumer fintech field RCT showing enrollment shifts can be decoupled from the outcome that matters (amount), squarely testing the brief's 'vanity metric' concern.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a behavioral nudge in a real consumer financial product can shift the metric it targets (autopay enrollment) while leaving the substantive outcome (debt/amount) unchanged or even producing side effects (missed payments) — a direct warning against equating enrollment with the amount actually saved.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment on live credit card accounts with 6-month follow-up on payment behavior and debt outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- debt paydown, not savings accumulation
- cardholders with liquidity constraints may not generalize to round-up savers

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments (including a 5-country, n=4207 study), default nudges shift risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes, but implementing a default — especially one people refuse — lowers users' rating of the advice and perceived honesty of the provider; framing of the default question also shifts amounts invested.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). European, consumer-facing experimental evidence on both anchoring by presets and the perceived-manipulation cost of defaults — matches two of the brief's core questions.

**Why it matters here.** Gives quantitative evidence that a pre-set level can anchor the amount chosen (C3) and that defaults carry a real reputational/trust cost when refused — directly informs whether a pre-set multiplier risks reading as manipulative in the EU market this app operates in.

**Method.** Multi-study experimental design with a large representative multi-country European sample; manipulates default presence and framing.

**Limitations.**

- financial advice/wealth management context, not a round-up savings app specifically
- no long-term follow-up on retention

## 3. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for downstream (delayed) default effects on consumer behavior, identifying time course of choice/consumption, antecedent preferences, and trade-off salience as the key moderators of whether default effects persist or fade.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). The only review-level synthesis specifically about when and why default effects persist or fade downstream, matching the brief's persistence question.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a structured way to predict and measure whether the round-up default's enrollment effect will persist or decay — directly useful for deciding what to monitor at 6-12 months rather than a single enrollment snapshot.

**Method.** Conceptual synthesis and framework paper reviewing consumer-behavior literature on defaults; abstract-only for specifics.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical framework, no original effect sizes
- not specific to financial products

## 4. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale U.K. bank field experiments show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts cut unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in market-wide annual savings, though consumers still only captured less than half of available savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). A UK consumer-bank automatic-enrollment field experiment with real effect sizes, directly on point for the entry-mechanism decision.

**Why it matters here.** The closest available analogue to our exact decision: automatic (opt-out) enrollment into a helpful banking feature inside a live consumer app, with quantified financial benefit and evidence that even a good default only captures part of the possible gain.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments run by major U.K. retail banks on default enrollment into alert features.

**Limitations.**

- measures alert engagement/charge reduction rather than a savings/round-up pot specifically
- no explicit persistence data beyond the experiment period
- no anchoring-on-preset-level analysis

## 5. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model of 8.6 million tipping decisions finds default adherence arises from the interaction of deviation cost and opt-out cost (84% from their complementarity), and that adding an intermediate preset option (15% alongside 20/25/30%) captures 69% of achievable welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Explicit structural method for how preset menu options anchor consumer choices, transferable to designing the round-up multiplier menu.

**Why it matters here.** The deviation-cost/opt-out-cost framework is a direct method transfer to designing the round-up multiplier menu: it shows how changing which options are preset reshapes chosen levels without changing underlying preferences, which is exactly the anchoring mechanism (C3) the level decision turns on.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on large-scale transaction data plus an independent stated-preference survey; counterfactual simulation of alternative preset menus.

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions, not savings contributions
- no persistence/retention outcome measured

## 6. Regulating Dark Patterns

Martin Brenncke · 2023 · Aston Publications Explorer (Aston University) · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2310.00340>

**Key finding.** Maps EU laws addressing dark patterns (subscription traps, hidden fees, pre-selected defaults, nagging, drip pricing) and develops a six-category taxonomy of autonomy violations to distinguish acceptable choice-architecture influence from regulable manipulation.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). The EU-specific framework for judging manipulative vs. helpful defaults, central to the brief's stated reputational-risk concern.

**Why it matters here.** Provides the specific EU legal framework for judging whether pre-selecting opt-out enrollment or a pre-set multiplier would read as an autonomy-violating dark pattern versus a legitimate default — directly shapes which entry/level design is defensible in this jurisdiction.

**Method.** Doctrinal legal analysis and taxonomy-building based on EU law and regulatory guidance.

**Limitations.**

- legal/doctrinal analysis, not an empirical behavioral study
- does not quantify enrollment or persistence effects

## 7. Automatic Enrollment with a 12% Default Contribution Rate

John Beshears, Ruofei Guo, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31601>

**Key finding.** At a 12% default contribution rate, 25% of employees had not opted out after 12 months (vs ~50% at lower defaults reported in prior literature), and many stuck to a rate that was likely suboptimal for them.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Strong anchoring evidence for the preset-level decision (C3), though setting is an employer plan.

**Why it matters here.** Direct evidence that a pre-set level anchors people onto a rate that may not match their financial capacity, exactly the anchoring risk the round-up multiplier decision faces.

**Method.** Administrative panel analysis of a single employer's 401(k) plan with an unusually high default rate.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored retirement plan, not a consumer app
- no active-choice comparison arm

## 8. The Behavioral Foundations of Default Effects: Theory and Evidence from Medicare Part D

Zarek Brot-Goldberg, Timothy Layton, Boris Vabson, Adelina Yanyue Wang · 2023 · American Economic Review · rct · overall 2/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210013>

**Key finding.** Default enrollment in Medicare Part D has large, persistent effects on enrollment and drug use, and beneficiaries remain passive even when the default option causes substantial losses.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best evidence that default persistence does not imply good outcomes for the defaulted, informing the enrollment-vs-welfare trade-off.

**Why it matters here.** Directly challenges the 'defaults always help' premise: passivity persists even when the default is costly, which is the strongest evidence for the support team's worry that passively-enrolled users are not well served.

**Method.** Two natural experiments plus a randomized assignment of default options; a third natural experiment examines drivers of active choice.

**Limitations.**

- health insurance enrollment for Medicare beneficiaries, not a consumer banking app
- no data on withdrawal or disengagement from a savings product specifically

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A payment-reminder nudge in a Brazilian financial app cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised overdraft fees by 9%, with users who had a history of overdraft use experiencing a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Consumer fintech RCT showing heterogeneous, sometimes harmful, effects of an automated intervention - the clearest 'who is hurt' evidence in a directly comparable digital-banking setting.

**Why it matters here.** Directly demonstrates the brief's core distributional worry: a well-intentioned nudge can help most users while actively harming a vulnerable subgroup, which is the exact risk profile to check before defaulting everyone into round-up savings.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment with a financial management platform, comparing users who did and did not receive reminders, with subgroup analysis by prior overdraft history.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrollment defaults, in a credit-card rather than savings context
- no data on 6-12 month persistence
