## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouded the minimum-payment autopay option cut the fraction of cardholders paying exactly the minimum by 23% after six months, but did not significantly reduce credit card debt.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Consumer, self-directed, liquid financial product showing a default-like nudge changes nominal behaviour without changing the underlying amount, which is the trade-off the brief asks about.

**Why it matters here.** Directly answers the brief's central question — enrolment/behaviour change from a nudge can move the nominal metric (minimum-only payers) without moving the outcome that matters (debt), which is the exact 'vanity enrolment' worry support raised about the round-up feature.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit card accounts, six-month follow-up, mechanism analysis via liquidity constraints.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not a savings product
- shrouding is a different mechanism than opt-in/opt-out/active-choice enrolment

## 2. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults raised campaign donations by over $43 million but also increased refund requests by nearly $3 million, with the largest impact on small, inexperienced donors who did not compensate by changing donation amounts.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Same choice-architecture mechanism (pre-selected recurring financial default) as the round-up entry/level decision, with effect size and heterogeneity by user experience.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real-world analogue to a preset recurring-financial-commitment default: it quantifies both the enrolment lift and the harm (regret/refunds) concentrated on inexperienced users, exactly the distributional trade-off the brief is asking about.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across political campaign websites that added pre-checked recurring-donation boxes at different times.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a savings product
- no data on outcomes beyond ~months around the election cycle

## 3. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for when default effects persist or decay downstream, driven by time course of choice, antecedent preferences, and salience of trade-offs.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). General consumer-behaviour framework for downstream default persistence, directly usable to design what to measure post-launch.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a framework for predicting exactly the question the brief asks — whether the entry-default effect on the round-up feature will persist at 6–12 months or decay — and what moderators (salience, antecedent preference) to measure.

**Method.** Conceptual/theoretical synthesis and research agenda across the consumer default-effects literature; abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical synthesis, not a new empirical estimate
- not specific to financial products

## 4. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrollment and of default contribution auto-escalation; steady-state saving rates rise by only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of employees escalate on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that defaults durably raise saving, with real effect sizes, which is exactly the counter-evidence the brief asked the scan to surface.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of the support team's objection: default-driven enrolment lift shrinks sharply once turnover, withdrawal, and later opt-out are accounted for — directly bears on whether opt-out enrolment for the round-up feature is a real gain or a vanity metric.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job separation, balance withdrawal, and opt-out from auto-escalation.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated setting differs from a self-directed app

## 5. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Defaults on financial risk-taking have medium-to-large effects, but implementing a default nudge (especially one the client rejects) lowers ratings of the advisor's honesty and advice quality, and framing of the default question shifts outcomes.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C5 distributional and manipulation risk (3/3). European, consumer-financial, experimental evidence on when a default is perceived as manipulative — precisely the reputational-risk question the brief raises for opt-out design.

**Why it matters here.** Direct European consumer-financial evidence that a rejected default carries a reputational cost — exactly the dark-pattern risk the brief flags for EU consumer protection — and that how the default is framed changes uptake independent of its level.

**Method.** Three experiments including a representative five-country European sample (n=4207) on default and framing effects in financial advice.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management advice context rather than a savings-pot feature
- risk-taking outcome, not enrolment/contribution amount

## 7. Defaults and effortful tasks

Lars Behlen, Oliver Himmler, Robert Jäckle · 2023 · Experimental Economics · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1007/s10683-023-09808-8>

**Key finding.** Switching university exam sign-up to opt-out increased nominal sign-ups but had no effect on effortful task completion across many exams, and only helped exam passing for students who were already responsive to university communication.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly tests the brief's central worry — nominal enrolment versus effortful downstream behaviour — with a heterogeneity analysis of who benefits, even though the setting is academic rather than financial.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest available demonstration that a default can move the enrolment number while leaving actual effortful engagement unchanged for the majority — precisely the support team's disengagement objection, with a heterogeneity finding (works for already-responsive people, not others) that speaks directly to who is helped versus who is not.

**Method.** Two field experiments changing exam sign-up from opt-in to opt-out, comparing sign-up rates against downstream effortful outcomes (participation, passing) and testing heterogeneity by responsiveness.

**Limitations.**

- university exam sign-up, not a consumer financial product
- no direct financial-amount analogue to 'money saved'

## 8. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrollment into real-time text alerts reduced unarranged overdraft charges by 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges by 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in annual market-wide savings, though consumers still captured less than half of achievable savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly on-domain: bank-app automatic enrolment with quantified effect size, the closest analogue to the round-up entry decision.

**Why it matters here.** This is a genuine consumer-banking automatic-enrolment study (not an employer plan), giving a real effect size for opt-out design in exactly the kind of app-based financial product the brief is building, and shows defaults help but leave real value on the table.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments at major UK banks measuring the causal effect of automatic (opt-out) enrolment into alerts.

**Limitations.**

- overdraft alerts are an information nudge, not a savings-level decision
- no measurement of persistence beyond the experiment window stated in the abstract

## 9. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A randomized reminder nudge in a Brazilian financial app cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised overdraft fees by 9%, with users who had a history of overdraft use seeing a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Strongest available consumer-financial RCT evidence that a well-intentioned nudge can help most users while harming a concentrated subgroup, bearing directly on C5's distributional and manipulation-risk question for the opt-out design choice.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the support team's concern: a behavioural nudge that looks like a clear average win can concentrate harm on the most vulnerable subgroup, which is exactly the distributional risk the enrolment-default decision needs to be checked against before defaulting everyone in.

**Method.** Field RCT with a financial management platform in Brazil; reminders for upcoming credit card payments tracked against downstream checking-account fee outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not enrolment defaults or contribution-level anchoring
- Brazilian credit/overdraft context rather than EU round-up savings
- no measure of enrolment persistence or withdrawal behaviour

## 10. Enhanced active choice: A new method to motivate behavior change

P. Keller, Bari A. Harlam, G. Loewenstein, K. Volpp · 2011 · Journal of Consumer Psychology · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Active choice, and an 'enhanced active choice' variant that highlights the loss from the non-preferred option, motivated behavior change effectively as an alternative to automatic enrollment, tested in field experiments.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by foundational · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). Explicit method transfer: the active-choice and enhanced-active-choice mechanisms tested here are the exact onboarding designs the brief is deciding between (C6), regardless of the non-financial application domain.

**Why it matters here.** The closest prior work on whether active choice captures most of the enrollment benefit of a default without imposing one, and it proposes a specific enhanced-active-choice framing directly transferable to the app's onboarding screen for decision 1.

**Method.** Series of studies including two field experiments testing active choice and enhanced active choice, building on the Carroll, Choi, Laibson, Madrian & Metrick active-choice design.

**Limitations.**

- Field experiments in medication adherence and general behavior-change contexts, not consumer financial products
- No round-up savings, persistence, or withdrawal data
- Small-scale field trials
