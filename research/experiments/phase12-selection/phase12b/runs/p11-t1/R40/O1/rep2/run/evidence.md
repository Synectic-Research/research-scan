## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouds the credit-card minimum-payment autopay option cuts minimum-only payers by 23% at six months but does not significantly reduce debt, and it lowers autopay enrollment overall, increasing missed payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Consumer financial self-directed setting showing a default-like nudge changes nominal behavior without changing the real outcome, contradicting the 'defaults always help' premise.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests the brief's central worry: a choice-architecture change can move a nominal enrollment/behavior metric while leaving the real outcome (amount paid down / saved) unchanged, and can have side effects (missed payments) among liquidity-constrained users — exactly the disengagement/harm pattern the support team is worried about for round-up savings.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit card accounts, six-month follow-up on payment behavior and autopay enrollment.

**Limitations.**

- credit-card debt paydown, not a savings product
- US context, not EU
- does not test opt-in/opt-out/active-choice enrolment directly, only a shrouding nudge within autopay settings

## 2. The Effect of Choice Screens on Mobile Browser Usage: Evidence from the EU Digital Markets Act [UNVERIFIED — check manually]

Jesper Akesson, Kush Amlani, Raul Cepeda Suarez, Emily Chissell et al. · 2026 · Social Science Research Network · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** Following the EU Digital Markets Act's mandated browser choice screens, Firefox usage rose 113% on iOS and 12% on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual, with effects persisting at least 15 months and differing by rollout scope.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best available EU, at-scale, persistence-tracked comparison of active choice against defaults, central to the Entry decision.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest recent, EU-regulatory-context evidence that a mandated active-choice screen produces large, durable shifts away from a preset default, directly informing whether active choice can substitute for opt-out in the Entry decision.

**Method.** Difference-in-differences design exploiting the staggered EU DMA choice-screen rollout across iOS and Android.

**Limitations.**

- studies browser choice, not a financial product
- effect size may depend on rollout mechanics (new vs. existing devices) that may not transfer

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Across nine 401(k) plans, steady-state saving rates rise by only 0.6 percentage points from automatic enrollment and 0.3 points from default auto-escalation once job turnover, withdrawal on separation, and opt-outs from escalation are accounted for; only 40% of those defaulted into auto-escalation actually escalate on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that default enrollment reliably lifts real savings once realistic attrition dynamics are modeled.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of the support team's objection: default-driven enrollment gains shrink substantially once medium/long-run dynamics (withdrawal, opt-out, turnover) are modeled — directly bears on whether an opt-out round-up default would be vanity enrollment.

**Method.** Longitudinal administrative-data analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating turnover, withdrawal, and opt-out dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated turnover/vesting dynamics don't map onto a banking app
- no EU or fintech context

## 4. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a 4,207-person five-European-country sample, default nudges shift risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes, but implementing a default (especially one people refuse) lowers ratings of the advisor's advice, an effect also of medium-to-large size, mediated by perceived honesty.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU multi-country experimental evidence on when a default reads as helpful vs manipulative, directly informing the dark-pattern risk the brief cares about.

**Why it matters here.** Directly quantifies the reputational/manipulation risk the brief flags: a default that shifts behavior can simultaneously erode trust in the institution presenting it, which should shape how the round-up default and any pre-selected multiplier are framed and communicated in the EU app.

**Method.** Three experiments, including a large representative multi-country European sample, manipulating default presence and framing in investment/risk allocation decisions.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-manager advisory context rather than a self-serve banking app
- risk-taking/investment allocation, not a round-up savings mechanism
- does not report persistence beyond the immediate experimental session

## 5. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and theoretical framework for downstream (long-run) default effects, showing they depend on the time course of choice/consumption, prior preferences, and salience of trade-offs, and sets a research agenda for reconciling inconsistent findings.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). A 2025 consumer-behavior framework directly organizing the downstream-persistence question central to the enrolment-design decision.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a structured lens for exactly the persistence question the brief asks — which conditions make a default's effect last vs fade — and should shape what the team measures at 6-12 months rather than just enrollment at day one.

**Method.** Conceptual synthesis and framework paper drawing on the consumer-behavior default literature. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical/conceptual rather than a specific quantitative effect size
- not focused on financial products specifically
- no primary data of its own

## 6. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale field experiments show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft text alerts cut unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, worth an estimated £170-240 million market-wide, though alerts captured less than half of potential avoidable savings.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Closest real-world UK consumer-banking-app auto-enrolment evidence, directly analogous to the round-up feature's Entry decision.

**Why it matters here.** Demonstrates that auto-enrollment into a passive banking-app feature can produce real, measurable financial benefit at scale, closely analogous to auto-enrolling users into round-up savings, while showing defaults capture only partial potential benefit.

**Method.** Large-scale field experiments at major UK banks measuring downstream financial outcomes of an auto-enrolled consumer banking feature.

**Limitations.**

- measures overdraft alerts, not a savings-level enrolment decision
- no comparison of opt-in vs opt-out vs active-choice enrolment designs

## 7. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** In 8.6 million NYC taxi tipping decisions, 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of deviation cost and opt-out cost together (removing either alone collapses adherence), and adding an intermediate preset option (15% alongside 20/25/30%) captures 69% of the achievable welfare gain at a 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). Explicit structural-modeling and counterfactual-menu-design method that transfers to deciding whether and where to pre-set the round-up multiplier.

**Why it matters here.** The structural decomposition of why people accept preset menu options and the counterfactual analysis of adding an intermediate preset level transfers directly to the multiplier decision: it gives a method for testing whether adding a 2x-style intermediate option between 1x and 5x would capture most of the achievable benefit without the extremes.

**Method.** Structural model estimated on 8.6 million transaction records, validated against an independent stated-preference survey of tipping preferences, with counterfactual menu simulations.

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions, not savings or financial contributions
- US taxi context, not EU or banking
- welfare/counterfactual framework assumes a stated-preference benchmark that a savings-pot analogue would need to replicate

## 8. Do people like financial nudges?

Merle van den Akker, Cass R. Sunstein · 2025 · Judgment and Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1017/jdm.2024.32>

**Key finding.** In a pre-registered survey of 2,100 Australians, financial nudges framed around savings and targeting deliberate (System 2) decision-making were rated most favourably, with transparency having no significant effect on approval or perceived manipulation.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Tests perceived manipulation and approval of financial nudges, which is the dark-pattern risk criterion the brief explicitly flags.

**Why it matters here.** Directly addresses the EU dark-pattern reputational risk named in the brief: shows how framing (savings vs spending) and messenger (bank) affect whether a nudge is perceived as helpful or manipulative, which should shape the messaging around whichever entry design is chosen.

**Method.** Pre-registered online survey experiment; 36 hypothetical financial intervention scenarios; representative sample of 2,100 Australians; six dependent variables including approval and manipulation.

**Limitations.**

- Hypothetical vignettes rather than actual enrolment behaviour
- Australian sample, not the EU regulatory context named in the brief
- No measurement of enrolment, persistence, or amount saved

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A randomized reminder nudge cut credit-card late fees by 14% but raised overdraft fees by 9%; users with an overdraft history saw a net 5% increase in total fees while other users saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Directly evidences the brief's distributional/manipulation-risk question (C5): a nudge helpful on average can systematically harm a subgroup, the failure mode the brief explicitly wants to avoid building.

**Why it matters here.** Shows a behavioral nudge that looks beneficial on average can net-harm the exact vulnerable subgroup (those already prone to overdraft/liquidity strain) the support team worries about with passive enrolment, arguing the team should measure subgroup-level harm, not just aggregate enrolment, before defaulting everyone into round-ups.

**Method.** Field experiment with a financial management platform in Brazil randomizing payment reminders; abstract gives effect sizes on fee outcomes.

**Limitations.**

- studies payment reminders, not opt-in/opt-out/active-choice enrolment defaults
- Brazilian credit-card and overdraft context differs from an EU round-up savings pot
- no data on persistence beyond the experiment window
