## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the credit-card autopay-minimum option reduced minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because nudged users set autopay only slightly above the minimum and some lost automatic payment protection entirely.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). On-domain consumer financial nudge study showing default/nudge effects on nominal behavior diverge from substantive outcomes, directly contradicting the 'defaults always help' premise.

**Why it matters here.** Directly answers the brief's central question in the right setting: a consumer financial-product nudge changed nominal enrollment/behavior without moving the outcome that matters (debt/savings amount), and worsened outcomes for liquidity-constrained users — the strongest evidence for the support team's 'vanity enrolment' worry.

**Method.** Field experiment (RCT) on live credit card accounts with 6-month follow-up on payment behavior and debt.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not savings accumulation
- single nudge design (shrouding), not a direct opt-in/opt-out/active-choice comparison

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** In three experiments (including a representative five-country European sample, n=4207), default nudges strongly shift risk-taking with medium-to-large effect sizes, but a default nudge — and especially a refused default — lowers users' rating of the advice received, mediated by perceived wealth-manager honesty; how the default is framed and presented also shifts the amount invested.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer-financial experimental evidence directly on framing/anchoring of default levels and on perceived manipulation, matching the brief's regulatory concern almost exactly.

**Why it matters here.** Directly informs both open decisions: it shows a preset level's framing can anchor the amount chosen, and it gives EU consumer evidence on exactly when a default starts to read as manipulative and damage trust — the reputational risk the brief flags.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, including a large representative multi-country EU sample, on default nudges in investment/advice settings.

**Limitations.**

- investment/wealth-management context rather than round-up savings specifically
- no 6-12 month persistence measure, single-session experiments

## 3. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults increased campaign donations by over $43 million but also increased refund requests by almost $3 million, with larger effects on smaller, less-experienced donors who did not adjust the amount they donated to compensate.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Quantified evidence of both the enrollment-boosting power and distributional harm of a pre-checked recurring default in a real consumer digital product, closely mirroring the round-up feature's opt-out risk.

**Why it matters here.** A directly analogous consumer digital-choice-architecture default shows real enrollment gains alongside real regret/withdrawal signals concentrated among inexperienced users — the closest empirical evidence for the support team's disengagement and complaint concern about opt-out enrollment.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design exploiting different roll-out timing of prechecked recurring-donation boxes across 2020 U.S. campaign websites; large-scale transaction data.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not savings behavior
- no direct long-term retention measure beyond refund requests
- U.S. setting, not EU regulatory context

## 4. Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

K. Donkor · 2026 · Management Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05272>

**Key finding.** A structural model of 8.6 million taxi tipping decisions finds 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of a deviation cost and an opt-out cost (friction complementarity), and a counterfactual added menu option captures 69% of achievable consumer welfare gain at only 5.4% revenue cost.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C3 anchoring on preset levels (3/3). A methodologically strong consumer digital-choice-architecture study whose menu-design and welfare-cost framework transfers directly to the round-up multiplier decision.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a rigorous, transferable method for reasoning about the round-up multiplier menu decision: how a preset level's design trades off consumer welfare against provider revenue, and how much of default adherence is genuine preference versus friction — directly useful for choosing and defending the 1x/2x/5x menu.

**Method.** Structural econometric model estimated on 8.6 million real transactions plus an independent stated-preference survey of preferences.

**Limitations.**

- tipping decisions on a ride-hailing app, not a savings product
- one-shot payment choice rather than an enrolled, ongoing savings behavior

## 5. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for 'downstream' default effects, showing default effects on later consumer behavior depend on time course of choice/consumption, antecedent preferences, and salience of trade-offs, and are inconsistent across studies.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). The clearest on-domain review of when and why default effects persist or fade downstream, filling the brief's request for a synthesis paper.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the moderator variables (time course, prior preferences, salience) that should shape how we measure persistence at 6-12 months and who a default helps versus hurts — directly usable as a measurement checklist rather than a design choice.

**Method.** Theoretical review/conceptual synthesis (taxonomy and framework), Consumer Psychology Review. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- conceptual/taxonomic, not a quantitative meta-analysis with pooled effect sizes
- not specific to financial products

## 7. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale UK bank field experiments show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft alerts cut unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in market-wide annual savings, though achieving less than half of the potential avoidable savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). A large-scale UK consumer-bank field experiment on automatic enrollment gives a directly comparable effect-size benchmark in the same industry and regulatory environment.

**Why it matters here.** The closest real consumer-banking analog of automatic enrollment producing measurable behavioral savings, setting a benchmark for what an opt-out round-up design could realistically achieve and its shortfall from the ceiling.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments at major UK banks; abstract-only for statistics.

**Limitations.**

- studies overdraft alerts, not savings contributions
- no data on enrollment persistence or engagement depth over 6-12 months for the feature itself

## 8. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In an RCT with 220 participants, all tested nudges - including default options - significantly reduced willingness to contribute to voluntary pension savings relative to a control group, contrary to prevailing international evidence, with the largest backfire from positive-narrative and default-option nudges.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest available contradiction of the 'defaults always help' premise, directly answering the brief's explicit request for counter-evidence.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise enrollment or contribution, a rare empirical counter-example the brief explicitly asked us to weigh against the 'defaults always help' assumption.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey (N=220) testing social norms, framing, default options, and narrative nudges on Chinese voluntary pension-saving decisions.

**Limitations.**

- small online survey sample (n=220) measuring stated willingness rather than real financial behavior
- Chinese cultural/institutional context differs from an EU consumer app
- voluntary pension product rather than a liquid consumer savings app
