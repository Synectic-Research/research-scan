## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouds the autopay-minimum option cut minimum-only payments by 23% after six months but did not reduce credit card debt, and it raised missed payments by reducing autopay enrollment overall.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). A rare consumer, self-directed, liquid-money field experiment showing a default-like nudge can raise nominal compliance while failing the substantive goal and harming a liquidity-constrained subgroup.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the internal premise that steering enrollment automatically improves real financial outcomes: it shows a default-style nudge shifted nominal behavior without improving the underlying goal, and even created harm (missed payments) via liquidity-constrained users — exactly the disengagement/complaint risk the support team is worried about for round-up.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit card autopay enrollment, six-month follow-up, large administrative panel of cardholders.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not savings accumulation
- shrouding a repayment option, not an enrolment/entry decision per se

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a 4,207-person study across five European countries, default risk-allocation nudges produced medium-to-large effects on risk-taking but also lowered ratings of the advisor's honesty and advice quality, especially when the default was refused, and framing of the default question itself shifted allocation choices.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer financial-advice setting giving effect sizes for anchoring on preset levels and quantifying the reputational/trust cost of defaults, squarely answering both the level-anchoring and manipulation-risk questions.

**Why it matters here.** Provides EU-specific evidence that a preset allocation/multiplier can anchor choices strongly (informing the level decision) while also damaging perceived trustworthiness when the default is refused — a direct test of when a default reads as manipulative in exactly this regulatory context.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments including a large representative multi-country European sample, manipulating default presence and framing in an investment-allocation context.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management advice context rather than a round-up savings feature specifically
- risk allocation rather than savings-rate multiplier as the anchored quantity

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Across nine 401(k) plans, medium- and long-run dynamics (job separation, withdrawal on separation, auto-escalation opt-out) shrink the effect of automatic enrollment and auto-escalation to just 0.6% and 0.3% of income respectively, with only 40% escalating on the first date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise saving in the medium/long run, the single most load-bearing finding for the brief's persistence question.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantitative statement of the support team's objection: initial enrollment lift from defaults is real but substantially eroded over time by opt-outs and withdrawals, directly bearing on whether the round-up enrollment number will be vanity.

**Method.** Longitudinal administrative analysis of automatic savings policies across nine 401(k) plans.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated setting, not a self-directed banking app

## 4. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and framework for how default effects persist or fade downstream, identifying time course of choice/consumption, antecedent preferences, and trade-off salience as the key moderators of whether a default's effect lasts.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Directly targets the persistence-of-defaults question central to the brief, in a general consumer (not employer) frame, and proposes measurable moderators.

**Why it matters here.** Gives a structured way to predict when the round-up default's enrollment lift will or won't persist at 6-12 months, and what to measure (antecedent savings intent, trade-off salience) to distinguish nominal from real engagement.

**Method.** Conceptual synthesis and framework paper covering the consumer-defaults literature; research agenda, not primary data. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- theoretical/taxonomic, not an empirical effect size
- not specific to financial products

## 5. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults increased campaign donations by over $43 million but also increased requested refunds by almost $3 million, with the largest effect on small, inexperienced donors who likely did not intend to give recurringly.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Near-identical mechanism (opt-out default on a recurring financial contribution) with direct evidence of harm to naive users.

**Why it matters here.** Shows opt-out defaults on financial commitments can generate large nominal uptake while causing real harm concentrated among the least sophisticated users, exactly the vanity-enrollment/complaint risk the support team raises.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design across political campaign websites exploiting the timing of default rollout.

**Limitations.**

- political donations, not a banking savings product
- short observation window relative to the brief's 6-12 month persistence question

## 7. When Nudges Backfire: The Effect of Social Norms, Framing Effects, and Default Options on the Pension Saving Decisions in China

Yuming Dong, Zhaoyue Wang, Zhuting He · 2025 · Advances in Economics, Management and Political Sciences · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2025.bl30018>

**Key finding.** In a 220-participant RCT on voluntary Chinese pension saving, all tested nudges, including default options, significantly reduced willingness to contribute and share of income saved, with default and positive-narrative nudges backfiring most, varying by income, education, and gender.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). The clearest available contradiction of 'defaults always help', directly answering the brief's request for a paper pushing against that premise.

**Why it matters here.** Directly contradicts the brief's working assumption that defaults reliably lift enrollment or contribution, showing defaults can backfire, with effects that vary by demographic group.

**Method.** Randomized controlled trial via online survey testing social-norm, framing, default-option, and narrative nudges on voluntary pension contribution decisions.

**Limitations.**

- small online-survey sample (n=220) measuring stated willingness rather than real behavior
- Chinese cultural/institutional context differs from the EU app setting
- voluntary pension rather than liquid consumer savings

## 8. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** At-scale field experiments at major UK banks show automatic enrollment into just-in-time overdraft SMS alerts cut unarranged overdraft charges 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in annual market-wide savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Same consumer-banking auto-enrollment mechanism and institutional context as our product, with concrete effect sizes on a comparable feature.

**Why it matters here.** Same institutional setting and mechanism (auto-enrolling users into a helpful account feature inside a consumer bank's app) as our round-up decision, giving a realistic benchmark effect size for automatic enrollment.

**Method.** Large-scale randomized field experiments at major UK banks testing automatic enrollment into text alert features.

**Limitations.**

- studies overdraft alerts, not a savings feature, so behavior and amounts differ
- no measurement of long-term disengagement or opt-out from the alert feature

## 10. Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market

Paolina C. Medina · 2020 · Review of Financial Studies · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa108>

**Key finding.** A reminder nudge for credit card payments reduced late-payment fees by 14% but increased overdraft fees by 9%; users with a history of overdraft use saw a net 5% increase in total fees while everyone else saved 15%.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by foundational · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). RCT showing that a nudge in a consumer financial setting improves average outcomes while harming a specific vulnerable subgroup, directly informing the distributional-risk question the brief raises about defaulting everyone in.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the brief's 'who is helped and who is hurt' question: a well-intentioned choice-architecture intervention can net-harm the exact subgroup (already financially stretched, prone to overdraft/withdrawal) most analogous to the passively-enrolled, disengaged users the support team worries about — this should shape what heterogeneity we monitor post-launch, not just aggregate enrolment.

**Method.** Randomized field experiment with a financial management platform in Brazil, tracking downstream fee outcomes across credit card and checking accounts.

**Limitations.**

- Brazilian consumer credit/checking market, not EU round-up savings
- studies a reminder nudge, not an enrolment default or preset contribution level
- no data on persistence beyond the study window
