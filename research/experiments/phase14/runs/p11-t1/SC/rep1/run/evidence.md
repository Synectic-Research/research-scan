## 1. The Effect of Choice Screens on Mobile Browser Usage: Evidence from the EU Digital Markets Act [UNVERIFIED — check manually]

Jesper Akesson, Kush Amlani, Raul Cepeda Suarez, Emily Chissell et al. · 2026 · Social Science Research Network · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** Following the EU Digital Markets Act's mandated browser choice screens, Firefox usage rose 113% on iOS and 12% on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual, with effects still present 15 months after rollout.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best EU-regulatory precedent for active choice screens producing large, persistent shifts away from defaults in a mobile-app context, directly informing the active-choice option in the entry decision.

**Why it matters here.** The strongest evidence available that a mandated active-choice screen produces large, durable behavior change away from a default in a mobile app under EU regulation, directly informing whether active choice at onboarding could substitute for opt-out without the disengagement risk.

**Method.** Difference-in-differences design around the EU DMA choice-screen mandate rollout across iOS and Android.

**Limitations.**

- browser default choice rather than a savings/financial decision
- no direct measure of amount saved or engagement depth

## 2. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the minimum-payment autopay option reduced minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, with liquidity-constrained cardholders driving the null result.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest available consumer-financial nudge study separating nominal enrollment change from actual behavioral/financial outcome, the core trade-off in the brief.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests the enrollment-vs-real-outcome distinction in a consumer financial product: behavior changed but the substantive outcome did not, exactly the 'vanity enrollment' concern our support team raised.

**Method.** Field experiment on credit cards with six-month follow-up (NBER working paper).

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown differs from savings accumulation
- the nudge shrouds an option rather than testing opt-in/opt-out/active-choice framing directly

## 3. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a representative five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges produced medium-to-large effects on investment risk-taking but also lowered ratings of the advice received compared to no default, especially when the client refused the default.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best available EU evidence on when a financial default is perceived as manipulative, directly addressing the brief's reputational-risk criterion.

**Why it matters here.** Directly evidences the helpful-versus-manipulative trade-off in an EU financial-advice context: even an effective default carries a perceived-honesty cost, bearing on both the entry design and how a preset multiplier should be framed to avoid a dark-pattern read.

**Method.** Three behavioral experiments, including a large representative multi-country EU sample.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management investment-risk context rather than round-up savings specifically
- outcome measured via advice ratings, not actual retention or saved amounts

## 4. The Behavioral Foundations of Default Effects: Theory and Evidence from Medicare Part D

Zarek Brot-Goldberg, Timothy Layton, Boris Vabson, Adelina Yanyue Wang · 2023 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210013>

**Key finding.** Default rules in Medicare Part D have large, persistent effects on enrollment and drug utilization, and beneficiary passivity is extremely insensitive to the value of the default option, even when staying costs substantial drug consumption losses.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Closest analogue to a self-directed (non-employer) default showing persistence and distributional harm, the exact mechanism gap the brief flags.

**Why it matters here.** Shows self-directed defaults (no employer intermediary) persist and can actively harm passive low-income users who don't reassess a bad default — direct evidence for the brief's 'who is helped, who is hurt' question and for testing whether the employer-trust mechanism is necessary.

**Method.** Three natural experiments using random assignment to default options among Medicare Part D beneficiaries.

**Limitations.**

- health-insurance domain, not banking/savings
- low-income Medicare population differs from general banking-app users
- abstract gives no percentage effect sizes

## 5. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics undermine the measured effect of automatic enrollment and of default contribution auto-escalation; steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of auto-escalation defaults hold at the first escalation date.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Contradicts the premise that defaults reliably raise real saving, using quantified effect sizes over the medium-to-long run, exactly what the brief asks to weigh against the 'default everyone in' argument.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of our support team's objection: enrollment lift is real at signup but shrinks sharply once attrition, withdrawal, and opt-out are accounted for — a direct check on the 'defaults reliably lift real saving' premise.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job separation, withdrawal-on-separation, and auto-escalation opt-out. Abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- retirement plans rather than liquid consumer savings
- abstract gives limited breakdown by demographic subgroup

## 6. Opt-in or Opt-out? The Power of Defaults in Pension Enrollment Choices

Tabea Bucher-Koenen, Luisa Wallossek, Joachim Winter · 2026 · CESifo working papers · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.65864/lhwgnu4zpv>

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment for low-income employees in Germany's public pension system raised participation by 23 percentage points, though most individuals actively opt out, with the default effect stronger when incentives are high and knowledge of enrollment status is low.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Quantified default effect and heterogeneity evidence in a government (non-employer) default setting, the closest test of whether the 'trusted intermediary' mechanism is necessary.

**Why it matters here.** Provides a clean effect-size estimate of an opt-out default absent an employer intermediary and shows the effect is conditional on incentive salience and consumer knowledge — directly informs how large our own enrollment lift might be and for which users.

**Method.** Natural experiment on nationwide auto-enrollment policy using linked administrative and survey data.

**Limitations.**

- public pension context, not a discretionary consumer savings app
- concerns a specifically low-income population

## 7. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults on political campaign sites raised donations by over $43 million while also raising refund requests by almost $3 million, with the largest impact on smaller and less experienced donors.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest available evidence that pre-checked defaults on recurring monetary commitments increase both uptake and complaint-like backlash, disproportionately among smaller/inexperienced users.

**Why it matters here.** The best available real-world causal estimate of a default on a recurring financial commitment, including a direct complaint/backfire proxy (refund requests) and evidence the effect falls hardest on inexperienced, small-money users, precisely the disengagement/complaint risk our support team raised.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design exploiting the timing of default rollout across multiple 2020 U.S. campaign websites.

**Limitations.**

- political donations rather than a savings product
- no direct measure of long-term retention or withdrawal

## 9. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Using 41 million observations in Denmark, each $1 of subsidy spending (requiring active choice) raised total saving by only 1 cent, while automatic/passive employer contributions substantially raised wealth accumulation; ~85% of individuals are 'passive savers' unresponsive to subsidies but moved by automatic contributions, while the wealthier, more sophisticated 15% 'active savers' mainly shift assets across accounts.

**Why it made the cut.** closely-related · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Provides the clearest large-sample evidence that passive/default mechanisms move real saved amounts and disproportionately help less-sophisticated users, the exact distributional trade-off the brief asks about, even though the setting is retirement accounts.

**Why it matters here.** The strongest available causal evidence that automatic/default mechanisms move actual saved amounts (not just nominal enrollment) far more than active-choice mechanisms, and that the people helped by defaults are exactly the less sophisticated, passive majority — directly bears on whether enrollment lift is 'vanity' and on who benefits from an opt-out design versus active choice.

**Method.** Large-scale administrative panel data analysis of Danish savings accounts, comparing active-choice tax subsidies to passive/automatic employer contributions.

**Limitations.**

- retirement/tax-advantaged accounts, not liquid consumer savings
- Denmark's institutional context (mandatory pension infrastructure) differs from an EU consumer banking app
- measures subsidy-induced shifting versus automatic contribution, not opt-in/opt-out/active-choice enrollment design per se
