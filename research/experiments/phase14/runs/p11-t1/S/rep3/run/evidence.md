## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A nudge that shrouds the autopay-minimum option cuts minimum-only payment by 23% after six months but does not significantly reduce credit card debt, because liquidity-constrained cardholders shift toward slightly-higher autopay amounts or drop autopay and miss payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest available consumer financial-product evidence that enrollment-shifting defaults do not necessarily change the underlying amount, with clear distributional heterogeneity by liquidity.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests our support team's worry: a design change that clearly shifts nominal enrollment/behavior can leave the underlying financial outcome unchanged or worse for liquidity-constrained users — exactly the 'vanity enrolment' risk for round-up defaults, and shows anchoring near a floor value (minimum payment) can undercut intended gains.

**Method.** Field experiment on live credit card accounts with six-month follow-up; abstract-only for further design detail.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt paydown, not a savings product
- does not address opt-in vs opt-out vs active choice framing directly, only a shrouding nudge

## 2. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and conceptual framework for downstream (post-adoption) default effects, showing they depend on the time course of choice/consumption, antecedent preferences, and salience of trade-offs, and identifies gaps in current understanding.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Gives the moderator framework needed to interpret whether enrolment gains from a default will persist or evaporate, which is the brief's central open question.

**Why it matters here.** Tells us what to measure beyond the initial enrolment number — persistence, disengagement, and which moderators (preference strength, trade-off salience) determine whether a default helps or just relabels behavior; directly shapes what our 6–12 month measurement plan should track.

**Method.** Conceptual/theoretical synthesis and research-agenda paper; abstract-only for underlying study details.

**Limitations.**

- conceptual synthesis, not a quantitative effect-size estimate
- not specific to financial products

## 3. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Medium- and long-run dynamics substantially shrink the effect of automatic enrollment and default auto-escalation: steady-state saving rates rise only 0.6% of income from auto-enrollment and 0.3% from auto-escalation, and only 40% of defaulted employees escalate on the first date, with further opt-out later.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly supplies quantified evidence contradicting the premise that a default reliably and durably raises saving, which the brief explicitly asked us to find.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantified statement of our support team's objection: the enrolment lift from a default is real at signup but shrinks sharply once persistence and disengagement are tracked — the exact trade-off the brief asks us to resolve before defaulting everyone in.

**Method.** Longitudinal analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job separation, balance withdrawal on separation, and auto-escalation opt-out; abstract-only for full model detail.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated mechanism does not map directly onto an app opt-out

## 4. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across experiments including a five-country representative sample (n=4207), default nudges shift risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes, but implementing a default (especially one the person refuses) lowers ratings of the advisor's honesty and advice quality, and how the default choice is framed (asking to invest vs. leave in cash) also shifts the amount allocated.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly evidences when a default reads as manipulative and damages trust — the EU consumer-protection/dark-pattern concern the brief flags — in a consumer financial-advice setting.

**Why it matters here.** Directly speaks to the reputational and dark-pattern risk of defaulting users into a feature: a default that is later rejected or perceived as pushy measurably damages trust, which should shape how we frame and disclose the round-up default and exit path.

**Method.** Multiple lab and large-sample field experiments on default framing in a wealth-management/investment-advice context.

**Limitations.**

- investment risk-taking with a wealth manager, not a round-up savings app
- no long-run persistence data beyond the experimental session

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w35112>

**Key finding.** Mandated active-choice browser screens under the EU Digital Markets Act increased alternative browser (Firefox) usage by 113% on iOS and 12% on Android relative to a no-mandate counterfactual, with effects persisting at least 15 months.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C6 active choice comparison (3/3). Strongest available evidence that EU-mandated active choice produces large, persistent effects relative to defaults in a mobile consumer app context.

**Method.** Difference-in-differences design exploiting the staggered rollout of EU DMA-mandated browser choice screens across iOS and Android devices.

**Limitations.**

- studies browser choice, not a financial or savings feature

## 6. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment into just-in-time SMS overdraft alerts at major U.K. banks reduced unarranged overdraft charges by 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges by 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in annual market-wide savings, though alerts captured less than half of the achievable savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Nearly identical decision structure (default enrollment into a consumer banking-app feature) with measured, sizeable financial effect estimates.

**Why it matters here.** The closest available precedent to our own decision: quantifies how default (auto-)enrollment into a discrete consumer banking-app feature changes real financial outcomes, and shows that even a well-targeted default only realizes part of the potential benefit — a caution against assuming enrollment numbers translate one-to-one into value delivered.

**Method.** At-scale field experiments at major U.K. retail banks testing automatic enrollment into text-message alerts versus no alerts.

**Limitations.**

- studies overdraft alerts, not round-up savings enrollment
- outcome is charge avoidance, not saved amount
- U.K.-specific banking regulatory context

## 7. Do people like financial nudges?

Merle van den Akker, Cass R. Sunstein · 2025 · Judgment and Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1017/jdm.2024.32>

**Key finding.** A pre-registered survey of 2,100 Australians found people generally approve of financial nudges, with strong preferences for System 2-style, savings-framed interventions, while transparency had no significant effect on approval.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C4 consumer self-directed setting (3/3). Provides consumer-facing evidence on manipulation perception of financial defaults (C5), directly relevant to the EU dark-pattern risk the brief raises.

**Why it matters here.** Directly tests when a financial nudge is perceived as helpful versus manipulative, informing how we frame and disclose the round-up default to avoid the dark-pattern reputational risk the brief flags.

**Method.** Pre-registered factorial survey experiment presenting 36 hypothetical financial-intervention scenarios varying transparency, framing, and targeted cognitive system, n=2,100 representative Australian sample.

**Limitations.**

- hypothetical scenarios, not an actual deployed product
- measures stated approval/likelihood of use rather than real enrollment or persistence
- general population, not specifically banking-app users

## 8. Optimal default options

B. Douglas Bernheim, Jonas Mueller-Gastell · 2024 · Journal of Public Economics · computational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2024.105203>

**Key finding.** A general theoretical characterization shows that when the designer and choosers agree on an activity's value, some form of weighted opt-out minimization (i.e., defaulting people in) is asymptotically optimal, with additional results for cases of disagreement, externalities, and normative ambiguity.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (2/3). Supplies the formal decision-theoretic logic for when an opt-out default is welfare-optimal, directly informing the entry-mechanism choice rather than just describing an observed effect.

**Why it matters here.** Gives the theoretical conditions under which defaulting everyone in is actually optimal versus not — directly answering when our opt-out design is justified versus when it risks exploiting disagreement between what we want and what users want, which is the core normative question behind the entry decision.

**Method.** Formal welfare-economics model of optimal default design, validated via numerical simulation of asymptotic optima.

**Limitations.**

- pure theoretical/simulation exercise, no empirical population studied
- abstract-only, no application to a specific financial product

<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.06.003>

**Key finding.** Active choice policies, and a novel 'enhanced active choice' design that highlights losses from the non-preferred option, can motivate behaviour change as an alternative or complement to automatic enrolment, especially where opt-out defaults are infeasible or ethically fraught.

**Limitations.**

- primary experimental application is medication adherence, not financial products
