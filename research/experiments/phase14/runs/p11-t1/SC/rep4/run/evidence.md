## 1. The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt

Benedict Guttman‐Kenney, Paul Adams, Stefan Hunt, David Laibson et al. · 2023 · National Bureau of Economic Research · rct · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w31926>

**Key finding.** A field experiment shrouding the autopay-minimum option cut minimum-only payers by 23% after six months but did not significantly reduce credit card debt, because nudged cardholders set autopay only slightly above the minimum and reduced manual payments.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Closest available consumer-product evidence that a default-style nudge can raise a compliance metric without improving the substantive outcome, directly bearing on the brief's central 'vanity enrollment' question.

**Why it matters here.** Directly shows a default-style nudge can shift a nominal enrollment/behavior metric without changing the real financial outcome — exactly the vanity-enrollment risk the support team raised for round-up defaults, and unlike most of this literature it studies a genuine consumer financial product rather than an employer plan.

**Method.** Field experiment on live UK credit card accounts with a 6-month follow-up.

**Limitations.**

- credit card debt repayment, not a savings product
- the mechanism is shrouding a minimum-payment option, not an enrollment default per se

## 2. Framing the Default Option Right

Luc Meunier, Yashar Bashirzadeh, Sima Ohadi · 2024 · Journal of Behavioral Decision Making · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2395>

**Key finding.** Across three experiments including a five-country European sample (n=4207), default nudges shift risk-taking by medium-to-large effect sizes but also lower consumers' rating of the advice delivered, especially when the default is one they refuse.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). EU consumer-financial evidence, with effect sizes, on when a default is perceived as manipulative rather than helpful — central to the brief's dark-pattern concern.

**Why it matters here.** Directly quantifies the reputational/manipulation cost of a default in a European consumer financial-advice context — exactly the dark-pattern risk the brief flags for the entry design decision — and shows framing of the default question itself changes outcomes.

**Method.** Three experiments; the largest uses representative samples across five European countries.

**Limitations.**

- wealth-management risk allocation, not a round-up savings multiplier
- no long-term follow-up on retention or amount saved

## 3. How default choice architecture impacts downstream behavior: A taxonomy, theoretical framework, and research agenda

R. Waisman, Tim Derksen, Gerald Häubl · 2025 · Consumer Psychology Review · other · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1002/arcp.70007>

**Key finding.** Proposes a taxonomy and conceptual framework for when default effects persist or fade downstream, identifying time course, antecedent preferences, and trade-off salience as key moderators.

**Why it made the cut.** plan-influencing · selected by score · strongest on C2 persistence and depth (3/3). Supplies the general framework for predicting persistence and heterogeneity of default effects that the brief's core trade-off depends on.

**Why it matters here.** Provides the conceptual vocabulary for the exact question the team needs answered — whether the round-up default's effect on enrollment will persist or decay, and for whom — framed at the level of general consumer decision-making rather than employer plans.

**Method.** Conceptual review and research-agenda paper synthesizing prior default-effect literature, abstract-only.

**Limitations.**

- conceptual synthesis, not new empirical effect sizes
- not specific to financial products

## 4. Do Ordeals Work for Selection Markets? Evidence from Health Insurance Auto-Enrollment

Mark Shepard, Myles Wagner · 2025 · American Economic Review · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20231133>

**Key finding.** Adding a minor enrollment 'ordeal' (extra step) to a health-insurance auto-enrollment policy reduced enrollment by 33%, disproportionately excluding young, healthy, and economically disadvantaged individuals.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Strongest available distributional evidence on the enrolment cost of adding friction, bearing directly on who is hurt by moving away from opt-out.

**Why it matters here.** Shows that adding friction (moving away from opt-out toward active choice/opt-in) has large, distributionally uneven effects on who stays enrolled, directly bearing on who is helped versus hurt by our entry-design choice.

**Method.** Natural experiment studying the removal/addition of an auto-enrollment step in a public health-insurance selection market, with a theoretical adverse-selection model.

**Limitations.**

- Health-insurance selection market, not a consumer financial savings product
- Adverse-selection framing does not map cleanly onto a savings feature with no insurance risk pooling

## 5. Defaults and effortful tasks

Lars Behlen, Oliver Himmler, Robert Jäckle · 2023 · Experimental Economics · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1007/s10683-023-09808-8>

**Key finding.** Switching university exam sign-up to opt-out increased sign-ups, but effortful downstream completion (exam participation and passing) improved only for previously 'responsive' students, with no effect for non-responsive individuals.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Best available evidence that opt-out defaults can inflate nominal participation without changing effortful downstream behavior for disengaged users, the core mechanism behind the brief's vanity-enrolment worry.

**Why it matters here.** Directly demonstrates the mechanism the support team worries about: a default can inflate nominal sign-up while doing nothing for engagement among people who were not going to act anyway, precisely the 'vanity enrollment' risk for round-up savings.

**Method.** Two field experiments changing exam sign-up defaults to opt-out at a university, tracking downstream effortful task completion.

**Limitations.**

- University exam sign-up, not a financial savings decision
- No amount-saved or 6-12 month withdrawal data given

## 6. Smaller than We Thought? The Effect of Automatic Savings Policies

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Jordan Cammarota, Richard Lombardo et al. · 2024 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w32828>

**Key finding.** Analysis of nine 401(k) plans finds automatic enrollment raises steady-state saving rates by only 0.6% of income and auto-escalation defaults by only 0.3%, and only 40% of those with an auto-escalation default escalate on the first date, with more opting out later.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Directly contradicts the 'defaults always help' premise with real numbers on how much smaller the effect is once medium/long-run dynamics are accounted for.

**Why it matters here.** The strongest recent quantitative evidence that headline default 'take-up' overstates the real medium/long-run effect on amounts saved — precisely the vanity-enrollment risk the support team raises for round-up defaults.

**Method.** Longitudinal administrative-data analysis of nine 401(k) plans incorporating job separation and balance-withdrawal dynamics.

**Limitations.**

- 401(k) retirement plans, not liquid consumer savings
- employer-mediated auto-escalation, no direct fintech app analogue

## 7. Sending Out an SMS: Automatic Enrollment Experiments for Overdraft Alerts

Michael D. Grubb, Darragh Kelly, Jeroen Nieboer, Matthew Osborne et al. · 2024 · The Journal of Finance · experimental · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13404>

**Key finding.** Automatic enrollment into just-in-time SMS overdraft alerts at major UK banks reduced unarranged overdraft charges by 17-19% and arranged overdraft charges by 4-8%, implying £170-240 million in annual market-wide savings, though users still captured less than half of the achievable savings.

**Why it made the cut.** design-changing · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest prior work: consumer retail-bank automatic-enrolment field experiments with quantified effect sizes in the exact UK/EU regulatory and market context.

**Why it matters here.** This is the closest available evidence to our own decision: automatic enrolment into a consumer banking app feature with quantified effect sizes, plus evidence that even a successful default leaves much of the potential benefit unrealized, directly bearing on enrolment vs. amount-saved.

**Method.** At-scale randomized field experiments run with major UK retail banks on an existing consumer banking app feature.

**Limitations.**

- Overdraft alerts, not a savings/contribution feature, so 'amount saved' logic differs from round-up mechanics
- No 6-12 month persistence data given beyond the trial window

## 8. Dark defaults: How choice architecture steers political campaign donations

Nathaniel A. Posner, A. M. Simonov, Kellen Mrkva, Eric J. Johnson · 2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218385120>

**Key finding.** Pre-checked recurring-donation defaults raised donations by over $43 million but also increased requested refunds by almost $3 million, with disproportionately larger effects on smaller, first-time donors who likely did not intend the recurring commitment.

**Why it made the cut.** contradicting · selected by score · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest empirical demonstration of a default-induced recurring financial commitment causing complaint/refund behavior concentrated among less-experienced users, mirroring the support team's objection.

**Why it matters here.** Directly demonstrates the support team's concern in a real setting: defaults on recurring financial commitments raise uptake but generate disproportionate refunds/complaints among naive users, the exact 'vanity enrollment' risk named in the brief.

**Method.** Staggered difference-in-differences design exploiting the different timing of default rollout across political-campaign donation websites.

**Limitations.**

- Political donations, not a savings or banking product
- No 6-12 month retention data beyond refund requests

## 9. For Better or For Worse: Default Effects and 401(k) Savings Behavior

James J. Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte C. Madrian, Andrew Metrick · 2001 · National Bureau of Economic Research · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.3386/w8651>

**Key finding.** Across three large firms, automatic enrolment raised 401(k) participation above 85%, but about 80% of participants initially accepted both the default savings rate (2-3%) and default fund, and half still remained at the default rate and fund after three years; automatic enrolment increased saving mainly in the lower tail of the distribution.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest available quantitative evidence on anchoring at a pre-set default level and its persistence over years, central to the level/multiplier decision.

**Why it matters here.** The clearest quantitative evidence that a pre-set contribution level anchors most people for years, directly bearing on decision 2 (whether a pre-selected round-up multiplier will anchor users away from a higher level they might otherwise choose) and on whether the enrolment lift is a net saving-amount gain or just a nominal one.

**Method.** Multi-year administrative panel data from three large U.S. firms comparing pre- and post-automatic-enrolment cohorts.

**Limitations.**

- employer-sponsored 401(k) setting rather than a self-directed consumer app
- three-firm sample, U.S. retirement context

## 10. Active vs. Passive Decisions and Crowd-Out in Retirement Savings Accounts: Evidence from Denmark *

Raj Chetty, John N. Friedman, Søren Leth‐Petersen, Torben Heien Nielsen et al. · 2014 · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · observational · overall 3/3

<https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju013>

**Key finding.** Using 41 million Danish observations, automatic (passive) retirement contributions raised wealth accumulation substantially, while active-choice tax subsidies induced mostly asset-shifting (each $1 of subsidy raised total saving by only 1 cent); ~85% of individuals are 'passive savers' unresponsive to subsidies but strongly moved by automatic contributions, and active savers are wealthier and more sophisticated.

**Why it made the cut.** foundational · selected by foundational · strongest on C1 default effect size (3/3). Closest and strongest available evidence quantifying the size and distributional heterogeneity of default versus active-choice effects on saving, a necessary reference point even though the setting is retirement accounts.

**Why it matters here.** The strongest available quantification of how much more effective passive/automatic mechanisms are than active-choice incentives, and of who the passive-responders are, directly informing the brief's 'who is helped, who is hurt' question and the credibility of the defaults-lift-enrolment premise.

**Method.** Population-scale administrative panel data analysis of Danish savings and retirement account activity.

**Limitations.**

- Danish retirement/pension accounts, employer-linked, not a self-directed liquid consumer app
- subsidy-based active choice differs from an active-choice onboarding screen
